Aviation unit conversions

Aviation mixes unit systems more often than many other fields. Airspeed may be in knots, runway length in feet or meters, temperature in Celsius, altimeter pressure in inches of mercury or hectopascals, and fuel in gallons or liters. The safest habit is to identify every unit before starting a calculation and convert all inputs into compatible units.

Speed

Aviation speed conversions covers knots, statute miles per hour, kilometers per hour and meters per second. Remember that converting the unit does not change IAS into TAS or groundspeed; those are different speed quantities.

Distance

Aviation distance conversions covers nautical miles, statute miles, kilometers, feet and meters. Nautical miles pair naturally with knots because one knot is one nautical mile per hour.

Temperature

Aviation temperature conversions explains Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin. Celsius appears throughout aviation weather and performance work, while Kelvin is useful in absolute-temperature equations.

Pressure

Altimeter pressure conversions connects inches of mercury, hectopascals, millibars and kilopascals. This is especially useful when comparing U.S. and international pressure references.

Fuel volume and weight

Volume conversions covers U.S. gallons, liters, quarts and imperial gallons. Weight conversions covers pounds and kilograms. Volume and weight are different quantities; fuel density is needed to convert between them.

Vertical speed

Vertical-speed conversions covers feet per minute, feet per second and meters per second. It also explains why FPM is different from climb gradient in feet per nautical mile.

Common unit traps

The most dangerous conversion mistakes are usually not difficult math. They are using imperial gallons as U.S. gallons, entering Fahrenheit into a Celsius field, mixing pounds with kilograms in a moment calculation, or comparing FPM directly with ft/NM. Labeling units at every step makes those mistakes easier to catch.

How Aviation unit conversions fits into pilot training

A pilot reference for aviation speed, distance, temperature, pressure, volume, weight and vertical-speed conversions.

Unit conversions are simple only when the starting quantity is identified correctly. Write the unit next to every value, convert one quantity at a time, and perform a reasonableness check before moving the number into another formula. A conversion changes the unit, not the type of speed, distance, pressure, temperature, weight or volume being described.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
