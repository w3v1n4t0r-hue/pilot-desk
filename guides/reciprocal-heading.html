Reciprocal headings

A reciprocal heading points exactly 180 degrees opposite another heading, course, or bearing. Pilots use reciprocals constantly: runway directions, reverse courses, holding-pattern orientation, procedure turns, wind discussions, and quick situational-awareness checks.

The add-or-subtract-180 rule

If the original number is less than 180°, add 180°. If it is 180° or greater, subtract 180°. A heading of 070° has a reciprocal of 250°. A heading of 310° has a reciprocal of 130°. This keeps the result within the normal 001° through 360° compass range.

Mental shortcut

Another quick technique is to add or subtract 200, then correct by 20. For example, 085 + 200 = 285, minus 20 = 265. The standard 180-degree method is usually simpler, but both are common mental-math approaches.

Runway directions

Opposite runway ends normally differ by 18 in their designators because runway numbers represent magnetic direction rounded to the nearest ten degrees with the last zero removed. Runway 09 points approximately opposite Runway 27; Runway 18 is opposite Runway 36.

Heading, course, and bearing are not the same thing

The reciprocal arithmetic is identical, but the meaning changes. Heading is where the aircraft nose points. Course describes an intended or actual path over the ground. Bearing describes a direction from one point to another. Keep the label attached to the number so a correct reciprocal does not become the wrong operational quantity.

Magnetic versus true

A reciprocal does not automatically convert between true and magnetic reference. If you begin with a magnetic heading, its reciprocal is also magnetic. Convert variation separately when needed.

How Reciprocal headings fits into pilot training

Learn how to find reciprocal headings and courses with the 180-degree rule, plus runway and navigation examples for pilots.

Navigation problems connect direction, wind, speed, distance, time and position. Keep true and magnetic references consistent, use groundspeed for movement over the earth, and preserve the sign of latitude, longitude and magnetic variation. A useful study check is to estimate the answer before calculating it, then ask whether the result makes sense for the route, wind and time available.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
