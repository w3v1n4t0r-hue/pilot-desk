Aircraft weight-and-balance envelope

A weight-and-balance envelope shows the combinations of total aircraft weight and center-of-gravity position that are approved for a particular aircraft configuration or phase of operation. Being under maximum weight is not enough if the CG is outside its allowable range.

Two axes, two limits

The vertical axis usually represents weight while the horizontal axis represents CG arm or percent MAC. After loading the aircraft, plot or compare the final weight and CG against the approved envelope.

Forward CG limit

An excessively forward CG can increase control forces and may reduce the ability to raise the nose under some conditions. It can also affect performance and landing characteristics.

Aft CG limit

An excessively aft CG can reduce longitudinal stability and alter stall or recovery behavior. The allowable aft limit exists for reasons that cannot be replaced by a generic “it feels balanced” judgment.

The envelope may change with weight

Some aircraft have sloping forward or aft limits, meaning the allowable CG range changes as total weight changes. Do not assume the same arm limits apply at every weight.

Fuel burn can move the point

As fuel is consumed, both weight and moment change. Depending on tank location, the CG can move forward or aft. Some aircraft require checking more than the takeoff loading point.

Use the aircraft-specific envelope

Generic limits are not valid. Use the current weight-and-balance report, POH/AFM and any approved supplements applicable to the actual aircraft.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
