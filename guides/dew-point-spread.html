Temperature-dew point spread

The difference between air temperature and dew point gives a simple indication of how close the air is to saturation. Pilots often watch the spread when considering the potential for fog, low cloud or changing visibility.

What dew point means

Dew point is the temperature to which air would need to cool, at roughly constant pressure and water-vapor content, to reach saturation. A higher dew point generally indicates more moisture in the air.

Calculate the spread

Subtract dew point from temperature using the same unit. If temperature is 18°C and dew point is 16°C, the spread is 2°C. If the spread narrows toward zero, saturation becomes more likely if other conditions support it.

A small spread is not a fog guarantee

Wind, cloud cover, terrain, moisture source, time of day and temperature trend all affect whether fog or low cloud actually forms. A close temperature-dew point pair is a clue, not a forecast by itself.

Nighttime cooling

On clear nights with light wind, surface temperature may fall toward the dew point. That can favor radiation fog when sufficient moisture is present. Strong mixing or cloud cover can change the outcome.

Cloud-base shortcut

The spread is also used in a rough convective cloud-base estimate. That shortcut assumes a simple vertical temperature and moisture profile and should not be confused with a reported ceiling.

Read the trend

A sequence of METARs showing a shrinking spread can be more informative than one isolated observation. Pair that trend with visibility, sky condition and forecast information.
