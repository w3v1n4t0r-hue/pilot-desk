Aviation distance conversions

Aviation navigation normally uses nautical miles, but performance charts, airport dimensions, terrain data and general maps may use feet, meters, statute miles or kilometers. The safest approach is to convert everything into one consistent unit before doing the next calculation.

Nautical mile

One nautical mile is exactly 1,852 meters. It is about 1.1508 statute miles and about 6,076 feet. Because one knot is one nautical mile per hour, nautical miles fit naturally with aviation speed and time calculations.

Statute miles

Statute miles are the miles used on U.S. roads. To convert NM to statute miles, multiply by about 1.1508. A 100 NM route is therefore about 115 statute miles.

Kilometers

Multiply nautical miles by 1.852 to get kilometers. A 50 NM leg is 92.6 km. To return to nautical miles, divide kilometers by 1.852.

Feet and meters

Runway lengths and obstacle dimensions can appear in feet or meters. One foot is exactly 0.3048 meter. One meter is about 3.281 feet. Do not use a nautical-mile conversion when the source is giving a vertical or runway dimension in feet.

Distance on a chart versus altitude

Horizontal distance and vertical height may use different units in the same problem. A climb-gradient problem, for example, may combine feet of climb with nautical miles traveled. Keep each unit labeled until the formula is complete.

Reasonableness checks

A nautical-mile value should be smaller than the equivalent statute-mile value and smaller than the equivalent kilometer value. If the number moved in the opposite direction, check the conversion.

How Aviation distance conversions fits into pilot training

Learn aviation distance conversions between nautical miles, statute miles, kilometers, feet and meters with pilot examples.

Unit conversions are simple only when the starting quantity is identified correctly. Write the unit next to every value, convert one quantity at a time, and perform a reasonableness check before moving the number into another formula. A conversion changes the unit, not the type of speed, distance, pressure, temperature, weight or volume being described.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
