Time, speed and distance for pilots

Time, speed and distance math appears everywhere in flight planning. It is used to estimate leg time, groundspeed, distance remaining, fuel burn and arrival time. The three quantities are directly related, so knowing any two lets you solve for the third.

The three core formulas

Distance = speed × time. Time = distance ÷ speed. Speed = distance ÷ time. For aviation calculations, speed is commonly in knots, distance in nautical miles and time in hours. Keep the units consistent before solving.

Worked example: time

If a leg is 90 NM and groundspeed is 120 knots, time is 90 ÷ 120 = 0.75 hour, or 45 minutes. Multiplying decimal hours by 60 converts the answer to minutes.

Worked example: distance

If groundspeed is 105 knots and the airplane flies for 24 minutes, first convert 24 minutes to 0.4 hour. Distance is 105 × 0.4 = 42 NM.

Use groundspeed for ETA

When estimating time over the ground, use groundspeed rather than true airspeed. Wind can make groundspeed very different from TAS. Rechecking the math with an updated groundspeed is a useful way to refine an ETA in flight.

Quick mental checks

At 120 knots the airplane travels about 2 NM per minute. At 90 knots it travels about 1.5 NM per minute. These simple relationships make it easier to catch a calculator entry that is obviously wrong.

Connect time to fuel

Once flight time is known, fuel used can be estimated from time × fuel flow. That makes time-speed-distance math a foundation for fuel planning as well as navigation.

How Time, speed and distance for pilots fits into pilot training

Learn time, speed and distance formulas for flight planning, navlogs, ETA checks and quick pilot mental math with worked examples.

Navigation problems connect direction, wind, speed, distance, time and position. Keep true and magnetic references consistent, use groundspeed for movement over the earth, and preserve the sign of latitude, longitude and magnetic variation. A useful study check is to estimate the answer before calculating it, then ask whether the result makes sense for the route, wind and time available.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
