Aircraft performance and weight & balance reference

Aircraft performance is a connected system. Weight, center of gravity, pressure altitude, temperature, wind, runway condition, and configuration affect different parts of the result. A useful study method is to separate the question into inputs, the physical relationship, the aircraft-specific chart or limit, and a reasonableness check.

Start with the aircraft and loading condition

Confirm the aircraft identity, current empty-weight data, equipment configuration, occupant and baggage stations, and fuel units. Calculate loaded weight and center of gravity with the applicable approved records; verify both against the correct loading envelope. A mathematically correct moment calculation can still be wrong if it uses stale arms, an incorrect fuel weight, or a different aircraft's limits. See the weight-and-balance guide, loading-envelope guide, and weight-and-balance workspace.

Atmosphere changes the chart inputs

Pressure altitude and outside-air temperature help describe the atmosphere used in performance calculations. Density altitude expresses the density effect in altitude terms, but it does not replace chart-specific pressure, temperature, wind, or runway inputs. A high density altitude generally reduces engine, propeller, and wing performance. Review pressure altitude, ISA temperature, and density altitude, then use the airplane's current performance charts.

Climb: connect airspeed, time, and distance

Vx is best angle of climb: greatest altitude gain for horizontal distance. Vy is best rate: greatest altitude gain per unit time. A climb gradient expressed in feet per nautical mile is also different from vertical speed in feet per minute. For a check, ft/NM = FPM × 60 ÷ groundspeed in knots. At 500 fpm and 90 kt groundspeed, the result is about 333 ft/NM. The example checks units and arithmetic; obstacle performance must still come from the applicable approved data and procedure.

Continue with Vx versus Vy, climb rate versus gradient, and obstacle climb-gradient math.

Turns, loading, and stall margin

In an ideal coordinated, level turn, load factor rises as bank increases: n = 1 ÷ cos(bank angle). At 60° of bank, the idealized load factor is 2. Stall speed in the same configuration varies with the square root of load factor, so the idealized ratio is about √2, or 1.41 times the one-g stall speed. This explains why a steep level turn raises stall speed; it does not provide an aircraft's approved maneuvering speed or a safe maneuver limit. See load factor and accelerated stalls, turn radius and rate, and maneuvering speed at weight.

Glide and runway performance need their own assumptions

A glide ratio describes still-air distance relative to height under stated conditions; wind changes distance over the ground. Runway contamination can reduce braking and directional control, and the tire-pressure hydroplaning estimate is not a landing-distance calculation. Use current runway reports and approved landing-performance procedures. Review glide-range assumptions and hydroplaning types and limits.

A repeatable performance-chart workflow

  1. Use current approved data for the actual aircraft and configuration.
  2. Identify chart units, weight, pressure altitude, temperature, wind, runway, and technique assumptions.
  3. Interpolate only when and how the chart or manual permits; do not extrapolate beyond its range.
  4. Apply required corrections and margins exactly as specified, then record the source and result.
  5. Recheck the selected weight and CG limits separately from takeoff or landing performance.
