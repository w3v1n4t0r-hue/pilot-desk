MULTI-ENGINE

VMC Factors for Multi-Engine Pilots

VMC is the minimum control speed with the critical engine inoperative under the conditions used to establish the published value. For multi-engine training, the important idea is not just memorizing the red line. It is understanding which conditions make directional control harder and therefore tend to increase VMC.

Training reference only. Use the current POH/AFM, approved checklists, instructor guidance and current FAA material for the airplane you are flying.

The big idea

VMC increases when the operating engine creates more yaw or when the airplane has less ability to oppose that yaw. It decreases when asymmetric thrust is reduced or when the airplane has more effective directional control.

Factors that tend to increase VMC

More power on the operating engine

More power produces more asymmetric thrust, so the rudder has more yaw to overcome. The FAA explains that VMC increases as power on the operating engine increases.

More drag from the inoperative propeller

A windmilling propeller produces substantial drag. That drag is on the failed-engine side and adds to the yawing problem. A feathered propeller reduces that drag and therefore reduces the asymmetric effect.

Aft center of gravity

Moving the CG aft shortens the effective moment arm between the CG and the rudder. With less leverage, the rudder is less effective at opposing yaw, so VMC tends to increase.

Lighter weight

For the certification relationships described by the FAA for many training twins, VMC increases as weight decreases. A heavier airplane has greater horizontal stabilizing tendency for the same asymmetric thrust condition. This is one of the counterintuitive VMC factors students are often asked to explain.

Less favorable bank

A small bank toward the operating engine helps the airplane oppose the yaw and sideslip created by asymmetric thrust. Reducing that favorable bank makes directional control harder and raises the speed needed to maintain control.

Density altitude and VMC

For a normally aspirated engine, available power decreases as altitude increases. Because the operating engine produces less asymmetric thrust, VMC generally decreases with altitude. At the same time, single-engine climb performance also gets worse. That distinction matters: a lower VMC at high density altitude does not mean the airplane will climb better on one engine.

Critical engine

In airplanes with both propellers rotating the same direction, one engine may create a more unfavorable asymmetric-thrust condition when it fails. That engine is called the critical engine. Some training twins use counter-rotating propellers, which can eliminate a single critical engine because the asymmetric-thrust geometry is essentially the same with either engine inoperative.

Why the published VMC is not a universal operating limit

The red-line VMC is established under specified certification conditions. Your actual control margin can change with configuration, power, loading, altitude, bank, propeller condition and other factors. That is why multi-engine training emphasizes recognition and recovery rather than treating one published number as though every real-world situation were identical.

Common oral-exam questions

What happens to VMC as density altitude increases? In a normally aspirated airplane, it generally decreases because the operating engine makes less power. Single-engine climb performance also decreases.

What happens when the CG moves aft? VMC increases because rudder leverage is reduced.

What happens when weight decreases? VMC tends to increase under the certification relationships described by the FAA for common training twins.

Why does feathering help? It reduces drag from the failed engine's propeller, reducing the yawing tendency.

FAA source

This guide is based on the FAA Airplane Flying Handbook, Chapter 13, Transition to Multiengine Airplanes. The FAA describes the certification conditions and explains how power, propeller drag, CG, weight and other factors affect VMC. Open the current FAA Airplane Flying Handbook.
