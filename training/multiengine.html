MULTI-ENGINE

Multi-Engine Study Hub

Multi-engine flying gets much easier to study when every aerodynamic fact points back to the same question: after one engine quits, can you keep control, reduce drag, and get the performance the airplane actually has?

Keep the multi-engine discussion tied to directional control

There is not a separate universal “multi-engine ACS” that replaces the certificate-level standards. The FAA private and commercial airplane ACS documents include multiengine airplane tasks. The Airplane Flying Handbook also has a dedicated transition-to-multiengine chapter. Use the ACS that applies to your certificate or added class rating, then use the exact airplane POH/AFM for speeds, limitations, systems, and procedures.

Know these relationships cold

  • What VMC means and why it is a certification/control concept rather than a target operating speed.
  • Why the critical engine matters on airplanes where one engine is aerodynamically more adverse to lose.
  • P-factor, accelerated slipstream, spiraling slipstream, torque, and the geometry that creates yaw and roll after an engine failure.
  • Why zero sideslip is not the same thing as “ball centered” in every one-engine-inoperative condition.
  • VYSE, single-engine climb performance, service ceiling, absolute ceiling, and the difference between maintaining control and maintaining altitude.
  • Feathering, windmilling drag, propeller governors, and the exact restart/secure procedures for the airplane you fly.

Oral-review prompts

Why can an airplane be controllable but unable to climb? Which changes to weight, density altitude, bank, power, and configuration move your margin? Why does a windmilling propeller hurt performance? What would you verify in the Seminole POH before quoting any speed on a checkride? If the engine failure happens just after liftoff, what is the first problem you must solve?

Use these sources first

The practical test is built from the FAA standard in effect on the day of the test. Start with the FAA Airman Certification Standards page, then use the current FAA aviation handbooks, the AIM, current regulations, and your aircraft documents. For PA-44 or any other training airplane, use the POH/AFM for that exact model and configuration. See the Seminole VMC study page for a model-specific study workflow that intentionally avoids inventing universal speeds.
