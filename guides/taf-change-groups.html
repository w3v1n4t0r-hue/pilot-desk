TAF change groups

A TAF describes expected airport weather over a forecast period. Change groups divide that forecast into periods when conditions are expected to become different, occur temporarily or have a stated probability.

FM — from

An FM group marks a relatively rapid change to a new prevailing set of conditions beginning at the stated time. The conditions after the FM group replace the prior prevailing forecast until another change group modifies them.

TEMPO — temporary

TEMPO describes temporary fluctuations expected during a specified period. Those conditions are not expected to prevail for the entire period, so they should be read as intermittent possibilities within that window.

PROB — probability

Some TAF formats use probability groups, commonly PROB30 or PROB40 where authorized, to express a 30 or 40 percent chance of specified conditions during a time window. Availability and exact use can vary by region and issuing practice.

BECMG

International TAFs may use BECMG to describe a transition occurring over a stated interval. U.S. pilots reading international forecasts should be prepared for coding conventions that differ from domestic examples.

Read timing in UTC

TAF validity and change-group times are expressed in UTC. Convert carefully to the airport's local time if that helps your planning, but retain the original UTC context to avoid date-boundary mistakes.

Forecast is not observation

A TAF describes expected conditions, while a METAR reports observed conditions. Comparing the latest METAR with the TAF helps show whether the forecast trend is developing as expected.

How TAF change groups fits into pilot training

Learn how common TAF change groups such as FM, TEMPO and PROB describe forecast timing and temporary or probable conditions.

Weather knowledge becomes useful when the observation or forecast is connected to a specific flight decision. Read the reported values in context, keep observation time and forecast period in mind, and separate what the weather product actually says from what a rule of thumb only estimates. When aircraft performance is affected, carry the current atmospheric values into the approved performance method rather than stopping at the weather interpretation.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
