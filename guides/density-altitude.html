Density altitude explained

Density altitude is pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature. In practical terms, it describes the altitude at which the airplane feels like it is operating because of the air density.

Why pilots care

Higher density altitude means lower air density. That can reduce engine power, propeller efficiency and wing performance. The airplane may accelerate more slowly, require more runway and climb less strongly.

What raises density altitude

High elevation, low pressure and high temperature all push density altitude upward. Hot summer days at already-high airports are the classic combination.

Pressure altitude first

A common quick estimate for pressure altitude is field elevation plus 1,000 feet for each inch of mercury the altimeter setting is below 29.92, or minus that amount when the setting is above 29.92.

Then account for temperature

PilotDesk uses a dry-air ISA atmosphere model rather than only the classroom “120 feet per degree” approximation. Even with a mathematically accurate estimate, actual takeoff and climb performance must come from the aircraft's approved performance data.

Worked example

Say an airport is at 5,000 feet and the altimeter setting is 30.12. The quick pressure-altitude estimate is about 4,800 feet. If the temperature is 30°C, ISA temperature near that altitude is only about 5°C. The day is roughly 25°C warmer than standard. Using the common 120-feet-per-degree shortcut puts density altitude near 7,800 feet. That estimate is useful for a reasonableness check; use the airplane's current POH or AFM charts for takeoff, climb, and landing performance.

Common mistakes

Watch the sign when correcting field elevation for altimeter setting, use the actual outside-air temperature, and do not treat density altitude by itself as a takeoff-distance answer. Two airplanes at the same density altitude can have very different performance because of weight, configuration, runway surface, wind, and aircraft-specific limitations.
