True versus magnetic heading

Navigation can reference either true north or magnetic north. The difference between them at a location is magnetic variation, and pilots must know which reference a chart, weather report, runway, heading or course is using.

True north

True directions are measured relative to geographic north. Great-circle calculations and many map-based computations naturally begin with true direction.

Magnetic north

Magnetic directions are referenced to Earth's magnetic field. In normal U.S. ATC phraseology, headings, bearings and wind directions are generally magnetic unless explicitly identified as true.

Variation connects the two

Variation is the angular difference between true north and magnetic north. It changes with location and slowly changes over time, which is why current chart information matters.

East and west variation

A common training mnemonic is “east is least, west is best” when converting true to magnetic: subtract east variation and add west variation. When converting the other direction, reverse the operation. Always confirm the convention used by the source or calculator.

Heading, course and track are different

Course describes an intended path, heading describes where the nose points, and track describes the actual path over the ground. Wind correction can make heading different from course even before magnetic variation is considered.

Runway directions are magnetic

U.S. runway designators are based on the runway centerline's magnetic azimuth rounded to the nearest ten degrees. That is why runway numbers can change after enough magnetic variation develops over time.

How True versus magnetic heading fits into pilot training

Learn true versus magnetic direction, magnetic variation and the sign convention used when converting headings and courses for flight planning.

Navigation problems connect direction, wind, speed, distance, time and position. Keep true and magnetic references consistent, use groundspeed for movement over the earth, and preserve the sign of latitude, longitude and magnetic variation. A useful study check is to estimate the answer before calculating it, then ask whether the result makes sense for the route, wind and time available.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
