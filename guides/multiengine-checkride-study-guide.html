Multi-engine checkride study guide

Multi-engine oral questions become easier when the topics are connected instead of memorized as separate definitions. VMC, critical-engine effects, zero sideslip, propeller drag and single-engine climb performance all describe what happens when asymmetric thrust changes the airplane's control and performance picture.

VMC and VYSE are not the same thing

Start with VMC factors, then review VMC versus VYSE. VMC is a directional-control concept demonstrated under specified certification conditions. VYSE is the published best rate-of-climb speed with one engine inoperative. The red line and blue line answer different questions, so being able to explain the difference is more useful than simply naming the colors.

Critical engine and PAST

Review critical engine and PAST. P-factor, accelerated slipstream, spiraling slipstream and torque help explain why loss of one engine can be more unfavorable on a conventional twin. Counter-rotating propellers can remove the single critical-engine disadvantage, but the airplane's own approved material controls the details.

Zero sideslip and drag

Zero sideslip is about reducing drag while maintaining directional control after an engine failure. A small bank toward the operating engine, combined with the required rudder, can reduce sideslip and improve performance. That is different from assuming wings-level flight is always the lowest-drag condition.

Recognize, identify, verify and feather

Use identify, verify and feather to review the training logic behind engine-failure recognition. Yaw, rudder pressure, engine indications and aircraft performance can all provide cues. Verification is critical before an engine is secured, and the actual sequence must follow the approved checklist and training for the aircraft.

Why a windmilling propeller matters

If you are reviewing windmilling propeller meaning, start with the drag: a windmilling propeller can create very high drag. Feathering aligns the blades closer to the relative wind and reduces that drag when the approved procedure calls for it. The performance difference is one reason propeller condition matters so much in multi-engine engine-out discussions.

Single-engine climb performance

Read single-engine climb performance and single-engine service ceiling. Losing one engine does not mean losing only half of the climb performance. Available power decreases sharply while asymmetric thrust and drag create additional demands, so the remaining climb capability can be small or even negative depending on weight, altitude and conditions.

Single-engine service ceiling

Single-engine service ceiling is a performance concept, not simply the airplane's maximum altitude with one engine operating. The exact definition and performance data belong to the approved aircraft source. For checkride preparation, be ready to explain why density altitude, weight and drag can change whether the airplane can maintain or gain altitude after an engine failure.

Accelerate-stop and accelerate-go

Accelerate-stop and accelerate-go answer different takeoff-performance questions. One looks at stopping after a decision point or engine failure scenario; the other looks at continuing. The available data and applicability vary by aircraft, so the operational answer must come from the current approved performance information.

Connect the concepts during oral prep

A useful way to study is to take one engine-failure scenario and explain the chain: asymmetric thrust creates yaw, rudder and bank establish control, propeller drag affects performance, the operating engine carries the remaining power demand, and density altitude and weight determine what climb capability remains. That chain makes the individual definitions easier to remember.

Multi-engine oral questions

What does VYSE mean?

VYSE is the best single-engine rate-of-climb speed. On many light twins it is marked by the blue radial line.

What is VMC?

VMC is the minimum control speed with the critical engine inoperative under specified certification conditions. It addresses directional control, not climb performance.

Why does a windmilling propeller create so much drag?

The airflow is driving the propeller-engine system, so energy is being taken from the airstream. Feathering reduces the aerodynamic torque and drag when the approved procedure calls for it.

Does VYSE guarantee a climb?

No. It gives the best available single-engine rate, but that rate can still be zero or negative depending on the airplane and conditions.
