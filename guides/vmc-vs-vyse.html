VMC vs VYSE: Red Line vs Blue Line

VMC and VYSE answer two different questions. VMC is about maintaining directional control after an engine failure. VYSE is about obtaining the best rate of climb with one engine inoperative under the conditions represented by the published value. Multi-engine students need to understand both because control and performance are not the same thing.

Training reference only. Use the current POH/AFM and approved procedures for the airplane you are flying.

VMC: the red line

VMC is the minimum control speed with the critical engine inoperative under specified certification conditions. It is primarily a controllability concept. Below the actual minimum control speed for the existing conditions, the rudder may not have enough authority to oppose asymmetric thrust and maintain directional control.

The published red-line value is not a promise that the airplane is controllable in every configuration at every condition. Actual control margin changes with power, propeller condition, CG, weight, bank, altitude and configuration. See the VMC factors guide for those relationships.

VYSE: the blue line

VYSE is the best single-engine rate-of-climb speed. It is marked by the blue radial line on many light multi-engine airspeed indicators. When the airplane can climb on one engine, VYSE is the published speed associated with the best rate of climb under the applicable conditions and configuration.

Blue line does not guarantee a positive climb. Weight, density altitude, configuration, aircraft condition and pilot technique can reduce single-engine climb performance enough that the airplane may only hold altitude poorly or descend even at VYSE.

Control versus performance

This is the distinction that matters most on an oral exam: VMC is about control; VYSE is about climb performance. An airplane can be above VMC and therefore controllable but still be below the speed that gives the best one-engine-inoperative climb. Likewise, flying at VYSE does not guarantee adequate terrain clearance or a positive climb.

Why pilots often think about blue line after an engine failure

After maintaining control and following the airplane's approved engine-failure procedure, pilots are commonly trained to establish the appropriate single-engine speed. In many training twins that means using VYSE as a key reference. The exact sequence, pitch target, configuration and checklist actions are airplane-specific, so the POH/AFM and instructor guidance control.

What happens as altitude increases?

For normally aspirated piston twins, available engine power decreases with altitude. That reduces asymmetric thrust, which tends to reduce actual VMC. At the same time, single-engine climb performance gets worse because the operating engine produces less power and the propeller and wing are working in thinner air. This is why a lower VMC at altitude does not mean the engine-out situation has become more favorable overall.

Common oral-exam questions

What is the difference between VMC and VYSE? VMC is a minimum directional-control speed under specified engine-out conditions; VYSE is the best single-engine rate-of-climb speed.

Which one is the red line? VMC.

Which one is the blue line? VYSE.

Does VYSE guarantee a climb? No. It identifies the published best-rate speed for one-engine-inoperative climb, but actual climb performance depends on conditions.

Can VMC change? The published red line is fixed for the airplane, but actual minimum control speed varies with conditions such as power, CG, weight, propeller drag, bank and altitude.
