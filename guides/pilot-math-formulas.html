Pilot Math Formulas: Aviation Formula Cheat Sheet

Looking for a pilot math formula or aviation formula? This reference collects the calculations pilots use most often for flight planning, training, and oral-exam study. Aviation uses a mixture of exact relationships, engineering models, unit conversions, and operational rules of thumb. They are not interchangeable. This reference identifies which type of relationship you are using, states the assumptions that matter, and links to a calculator only after the underlying math is clear.

Quick answer: the pilot formulas used most often are distance = groundspeed × time, fuel used = fuel flow × time, crosswind = wind × sin θ, headwind/tailwind = wind × cos θ, and the standard-atmosphere relationships used for pressure and density altitude. The sections below separate exact identities from pilot approximations.

How to read this reference

Time, groundspeed, and distance

Type: identity. For constant groundspeed:

Distance = Groundspeed × Time

With groundspeed in knots and time in hours, distance is in nautical miles. Since one knot is one nautical mile per hour:

NM per minute = Knots ÷ 60

Example: 120 kt = 2 NM/min. The identity is exact; the assumption that groundspeed remains constant over the interval may not be.

Fuel used and endurance

Type: model with a constant-flow assumption.

Fuel used = Fuel flow × Time
Endurance = Usable fuel ÷ Fuel flow

These equations are mathematically exact if fuel flow is constant and the fuel quantity is defined consistently. Real flights rarely maintain one fuel flow from engine start to shutdown, so operational planning may require separate taxi, climb, cruise, descent, alternate, reserve, and contingency segments.

Crosswind and along-runway wind components

Type: vector decomposition. If θ is the smallest angle between the wind direction and runway axis, and all directions use the same reference:

Crosswind magnitude = W × sin θ
Along-runway component = W × cos θ

The sine/cosine relationships are exact vector geometry. What can be wrong is the input reference. A true wind direction and a magnetic runway heading should not be mixed without conversion. The sign convention for “left/right” or “headwind/tailwind” must also be defined by the calculator.

Wind triangle: heading, wind correction, and groundspeed

Type: vector model. The airplane's air-velocity vector plus the wind vector equals the ground-velocity vector. The solution is exact for the entered steady wind, true airspeed, and desired course. Real wind varies spatially and temporally, so the calculated heading and groundspeed are planning values rather than a guarantee.

Pressure altitude

Type: standard-atmosphere reference. Pressure altitude is the altitude in the standard atmosphere corresponding to the existing pressure. Setting 29.92 inHg on an altimeter provides pressure altitude directly. A common mental approximation is:

PA ≈ Field elevation + (29.92 − Altimeter setting) × 1,000 ft

The 1,000-ft-per-inch factor is an approximation. Use the aircraft/performance-chart method when precision matters.

Pressure Altitude Calculator

ISA temperature

Type: atmosphere model. In the ISA troposphere, temperature decreases at approximately 1.98°C per 1,000 ft. Pilot mental math normally rounds this to 2°C:

TISA ≈ 15°C − 2°C × altitude in thousands of feet

ISA Temperature Calculator · ISA Temperature Chart

Density altitude

Type: atmosphere model; shortcut shown below is an approximation. Density altitude is the ISA altitude corresponding to the existing air density. A widely taught estimate is:

DA ≈ PA + 120 × (OAT − TISA)

The 120 ft/°C factor is useful for mental math but is not the definition of density altitude. A full calculation derives density from pressure and temperature and then finds the equivalent ISA altitude. Humidity may or may not be included depending on the model.

Climb gradient and vertical speed

Type: unit conversion for a specified geometric gradient. If the required climb gradient is in ft/NM:

Vertical speed (ft/min) = Gradient (ft/NM) × Groundspeed (kt) ÷ 60

This relationship explains why a tailwind increases the vertical speed required to satisfy a fixed climb gradient: the airplane covers more nautical miles each minute.

Three-degree descent

Type: geometric model. For a 3° straight flight path and constant groundspeed:

VS = GS × 6,076.12 ÷ 60 × tan(3°)
VS ≈ GS × 5.31

The common GS × 5 technique is a deliberate mental approximation, not the exact 3° geometry.

Load factor in a coordinated level turn

Type: force-balance model. If the airplane is coordinated and maintaining altitude:

n = 1 ÷ cos φ

Bank angle is not inherently “G.” This relationship exists because the vertical component of the tilted lift vector must equal weight in a constant-altitude turn.

Accelerated stall speed

Type: aerodynamic model. For the same weight, configuration, and maximum lift coefficient:

VS,n = VS,1G × √n

At 2 G the theoretical stall-speed multiplier is √2 ≈ 1.414. The formula describes the load-factor relationship; it does not replace published aircraft stall information.

Turn radius and rate

Type: coordinated, constant-altitude turn model. In consistent units:

Turn radius R = V² ÷ (g tan φ)
Turn rate ω = g tan φ ÷ V

These relationships use true velocity through the airmass for the aerodynamic turn. Ground track radius is additionally affected by wind.

Turn Radius Calculator · Rate of Turn Calculator

Pivotal altitude

Type: ground-reference geometry model. Combining coordinated-turn radius with the pylon sight-line geometry yields:

h = V² ÷ g

Converting groundspeed in knots to feet per second gives the familiar training form:

Pivotal altitude (ft AGL) ≈ GS² ÷ 11.3

Groundspeed—not indicated airspeed—is the relevant speed because the maneuver is referenced to a fixed point on the ground.

Moment, center of gravity, and percent MAC

Type: identities once the aircraft datum geometry is defined.

Moment = Weight × Arm
CG = Total Moment ÷ Total Weight
%MAC = (CG − LEMAC) ÷ MAC × 100

The equations are generic; the station arms, empty-weight data, MAC geometry, loading limits, and allowable envelope are aircraft-specific.

Dynamic hydroplaning speed

Type: empirical rule of thumb.

Vp ≈ 9 × √(tire pressure in psi)

This is a training estimate associated with dynamic hydroplaning susceptibility. It is not a runway-performance limit and does not predict braking action for a specific airplane, tire, runway surface, water depth, or anti-skid system.

What should not be reduced to a generic formula

Takeoff distance, landing distance, accelerate-stop/accelerate-go performance, single-engine climb, VMC, VYSE performance, aircraft-specific best glide, loading limits, and most operational limitations are not responsibly replaced by a universal web equation. Use the current POH/AFM, approved charts, regulations, and operator procedures.
