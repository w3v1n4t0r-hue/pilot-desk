Climb rate versus climb gradient

Climb rate and climb gradient both describe a climb, but they measure different things. Climb rate is vertical distance per time; climb gradient is vertical distance per horizontal distance. Groundspeed connects the two. Keeping that distinction clear matters when interpreting an obstacle requirement or comparing a charted gradient with a vertical-speed indication.

Definitions and formulas

Climb rate is usually shown in feet per minute (FPM). Gradient is commonly shown in feet per nautical mile (ft/NM). Because knots mean nautical miles per hour:

FPM = gradient (ft/NM) × groundspeed (kt) ÷ 60

Gradient (ft/NM) = FPM × 60 ÷ groundspeed (kt)

Use groundspeed in these equations, not indicated airspeed. The gradient describes progress over the ground, so wind affects how much vertical speed is needed to maintain a fixed gradient.

Worked example in both directions

A required gradient of 300 ft/NM at 120 kt groundspeed requires 600 FPM: 300 × 120 ÷ 60 = 600. If groundspeed rises to 140 kt, the same ground gradient requires 700 FPM. In the reverse direction, 800 FPM at 120 kt produces 400 ft/NM: 800 × 60 ÷ 120 = 400.

Check the scale before trusting the arithmetic. At 120 kt, the airplane covers 2 NM each minute, so a 300 ft/NM gradient requires 300 × 2 = 600 ft each minute. That mental check should agree with the formula.

Gradient, angle, and percent are related but different units

A gradient of 300 ft/NM is approximately 4.94 percent because one nautical mile is about 6,076 ft. The corresponding angle is about 2.83°: angle = arctan(300 ÷ 6,076). These values describe the same geometry in different forms, but a published departure or approach requirement should be used in the units and context in which it is provided.

What the calculation can and cannot establish

A conversion tells you the vertical rate that corresponds to a selected groundspeed and gradient. It does not show that a particular airplane can achieve that rate under the current weight, pressure altitude, temperature, configuration, or procedure. Compare the requirement with the applicable approved aircraft performance data, and consider the procedure and operating requirements that apply.

A headwind lowers groundspeed and therefore lowers the FPM needed for the same ground gradient; a tailwind raises it. The airplane's air-relative climb capability still depends on its performance and condition.
