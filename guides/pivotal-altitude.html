Pivotal altitude explained

Pivotal altitude is the altitude at which, during a properly performed ground-reference maneuver, a selected visual reference appears to remain fixed relative to a point on the wing as the airplane turns.

The common rule

Pivotal altitude (ft AGL) ≈ Groundspeed² ÷ 11.3

Because groundspeed is squared, small speed changes produce larger altitude changes. At 100 knots groundspeed, the estimate is about 885 feet AGL.

Why wind matters

The airplane's groundspeed changes around the maneuver when wind is present, so pivotal altitude is not necessarily constant through the entire turn. Higher groundspeed requires a higher pivotal altitude; lower groundspeed requires a lower one.

Use groundspeed, not indicated airspeed

The visual geometry is tied to movement over the ground, which is why the rule uses groundspeed. GPS groundspeed can be useful for training, but pilots still need to fly the maneuver visually and in accordance with their training and applicable standards.

Worked examples

Using the common knots formula, 100 knots groundspeed gives about 885 feet AGL because 100² ÷ 11.3 is about 885. At 120 knots, the result rises to about 1,274 feet AGL. The squared term matters: a modest groundspeed increase produces a much larger pivotal-altitude increase.

Why wind changes the picture

Pivotal altitude depends on groundspeed, not indicated airspeed. During a maneuver around a point, groundspeed changes as the airplane moves between upwind and downwind portions. That means the pivotal altitude is not one fixed number for the entire maneuver. The calculator gives a starting estimate for a particular groundspeed; use the visual relationship, aircraft limitations, and instructor guidance while flying the maneuver.

Keep the units straight

The common 11.3 divisor is tied to groundspeed in knots and an answer in feet AGL. Changing the speed unit changes the constant. It is also easy to accidentally use indicated airspeed when the maneuver depends on groundspeed. If the wind changes your groundspeed around the point, the pivotal altitude changes with it, which is why the visual reference remains more important than memorizing one fixed altitude.

How Pivotal altitude explained fits into pilot training

Learn pivotal altitude, why it changes with groundspeed, and how to use the common GS squared divided by 11.3 rule.

Aircraft-performance math shows how variables are related, but the operational answer remains aircraft specific. Weight, configuration, runway condition, wind, pressure, temperature and technique can all change the result. Use the relationship to understand the trend and to check arithmetic, then use the current POH or AFM procedure and chart for the airplane when the number affects a flight.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
