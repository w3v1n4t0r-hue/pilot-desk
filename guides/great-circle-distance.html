Great-circle distance for pilots

A great-circle route follows the shortest path between two points on the surface of a sphere. Because Earth is curved, the shortest route between two distant airports is not always the straight-looking line you might expect on a flat map.

Latitude and longitude inputs

Great-circle calculations use the latitude and longitude of the departure and destination points. The coordinate difference is converted into angular separation, which is then turned into distance using an Earth-radius model.

Why long routes curve on maps

Many map projections distort distance or direction. A great-circle route may appear curved on a chart even though it is the shortest path over the globe.

Initial bearing

A great-circle calculator can also estimate the initial bearing from the first point toward the second. The bearing changes along a long great-circle route, so the initial value is not necessarily the heading you would hold for the entire flight.

Useful for planning, not navigation authority

This math is helpful for rough route comparisons, training, and understanding spherical navigation. Real flight planning must account for airways, airspace, weather, winds, procedures, terrain, and current navigation data.
