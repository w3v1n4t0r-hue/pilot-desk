What this wind triangle solves

The calculator finds the true heading needed to hold a planned true course, plus the resulting wind correction angle and groundspeed. Use it to understand wind drift or cross-check a leg in a navlog; it does not provide a magnetic heading unless you separately apply variation.

Vector relationships

Crosswind component = wind speed × sin(relative wind angle)
Headwind component = wind speed × cos(relative wind angle)

The calculator resolves the complete vectors to find the correction and groundspeed; these components make the direction and approximate effect easier to check.

Inputs and references

Enter planned course and wind direction as true degrees, true airspeed in knots, and wind speed in knots. Aviation wind direction is the direction the wind comes from. A magnetic course paired with a true wind creates a reference error, so convert consistently.

Worked example

For course 090°T, TAS 120 kt, and wind from 020°T at 20 kt, the wind is 70° left of course. Crosswind is 20 × sin 70° = 18.8 kt from the left; headwind is 20 × cos 70° = 6.8 kt. The exact correction is about 9° left, giving heading 081°T and groundspeed about 112 kt. Groundspeed is lower than TAS because of the headwind component.

Heading is the direction the nose points; course is the intended ground path; track is the path actually flown. If wind changes, track, groundspeed, time, and fuel can all change. The route planner uses the wind entered for its navlog; its endpoint METAR is airport context, not an enroute forecast.

The calculator resolves vectors and rejects combinations with no steady solution, such as a crosswind component that the entered TAS cannot overcome. Values are planning estimates: forecast accuracy, altitude, and actual aircraft performance matter. See the wind triangle lesson, Route Planner, the FAA Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, and PilotDesk sources and methods.

Wind Triangle questions

What does a wind triangle solve?

It combines course, true airspeed and wind to solve the heading needed to hold course, the resulting groundspeed and the wind-correction angle.

Why must direction references be consistent?

A true course should be paired with a true wind reference, while magnetic values should be paired consistently. Mixing references can introduce an error roughly equal to local magnetic variation.

What if the entered wind is stronger than the airplane can overcome?

Some combinations have no steady solution for the requested course. PilotDesk warns when the entered crosswind relationship makes the wind-triangle solution impossible.
