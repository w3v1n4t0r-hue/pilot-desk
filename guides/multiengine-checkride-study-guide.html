MULTI-ENGINE

Multi-Engine Checkride Study Guide

This hub brings together the concepts that show up repeatedly in commercial multi-engine training and oral exams. The goal is not to memorize disconnected definitions. It is to understand how asymmetric thrust, directional control, propeller drag and single-engine performance fit together.

Training reference only. Use the current POH/AFM, approved checklist, instructor guidance and current FAA material for the airplane you fly.

Core control-speed topics

Start with VMC factors, then review VMC versus VYSE. VMC is about directional control with the critical engine inoperative under specified conditions. VYSE is the published best rate-of-climb speed with one engine inoperative. They answer different questions, which is why confusing the red line and blue line is a common oral-exam mistake.

Critical engine and PAST

Review critical engine and PAST. P-factor, accelerated slipstream, spiraling slipstream and torque explain why an engine can be more critical on some conventional twins. Counter-rotating propellers can remove the single critical-engine disadvantage.

Zero sideslip and drag

Zero sideslip is about minimizing drag while maintaining directional control after an engine failure. A small bank toward the operating engine can reduce sideslip and improve performance. That is different from simply flying wings level with rudder pressure.

Engine failure recognition

Study identify, verify and feather as a training concept. Recognition is built around aircraft yaw, rudder pressure and performance cues. Verification must follow the approved aircraft procedure before an engine is secured. Feathering reduces the enormous drag produced by a windmilling propeller.

Single-engine performance

Read single-engine climb performance and single-engine service ceiling. Losing one engine does not mean losing only half the climb performance; the loss is much greater because power available drops while drag and asymmetric-control demands increase.

Takeoff decision concepts

Accelerate-stop and accelerate-go describe two very different performance questions after an engine failure during takeoff. The actual numbers and procedures are aircraft-specific and must come from approved performance data.

FAA source

These topics are covered in the FAA Airplane Flying Handbook, especially the chapter on transition to multiengine airplanes. Open the current FAA Airplane Flying Handbook.
