DME arc distance

A DME arc keeps the aircraft at approximately a constant slant-range distance from a DME facility while the bearing to the station changes. The path is part of a circle, so arc distance depends on both the radius and the number of degrees flown around that circle.

Arc length formula

Arc distance = radius × angle in radians. Because pilots normally think in degrees, convert degrees to radians by multiplying degrees by π ÷ 180.

Worked example

For a 10 DME arc covering 60 degrees, the distance is 10 × (60 × π ÷ 180), which is about 10.47 NM. A larger arc angle or larger radius creates a longer path.

A full circle check

A 360-degree arc at 10 NM radius would be the circumference of the circle: 2 × π × 10, or about 62.8 NM. A 90-degree arc is one quarter of that distance.

DME shows slant range

DME measures line-of-sight slant range rather than pure horizontal ground distance. Close to the station and high above it, the difference can be noticeable. The simple arc-length calculation treats the stated radius geometrically.

Arc distance is not the same as radial change

Changing from one radial to another tells you the angle swept around the station. The distance flown depends on the radius at which that angle is flown.

Procedure use

When a DME arc is part of an instrument procedure, the published procedure and current navigation data control. The formula is a training aid for understanding geometry, not a substitute for procedure guidance.

How DME arc distance fits into pilot training

Learn how DME arc radius and degrees flown determine arc distance, with pilot examples and instrument-training context.

Navigation problems connect direction, wind, speed, distance, time and position. Keep true and magnetic references consistent, use groundspeed for movement over the earth, and preserve the sign of latitude, longitude and magnetic variation. A useful study check is to estimate the answer before calculating it, then ask whether the result makes sense for the route, wind and time available.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
