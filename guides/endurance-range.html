Aircraft endurance and range math

Endurance answers how long an airplane can remain airborne with the fuel available. Range answers how far it can travel. The two are related, but wind can change range without changing the fuel-flow-based endurance calculation.

Endurance formula

A basic planning estimate is usable fuel ÷ fuel flow = endurance. If 36 usable gallons are available and cruise fuel flow is 9 gallons per hour, the mathematical endurance is 4 hours before accounting for reserve, climb, taxi, mixture changes or other operating factors.

Subtract reserve before finding usable trip time

If you plan to protect 9 gallons as reserve, only 27 gallons are available for the trip in this simplified example. At 9 GPH, that gives 3 hours of planned trip endurance. Reserve requirements and prudent personal minimums are operational decisions, not something a basic arithmetic result can replace.

Range formula

Estimated range equals endurance multiplied by groundspeed. Three hours at 110 knots groundspeed gives about 330 NM. A headwind that lowers groundspeed to 90 knots reduces that estimate to 270 NM even if fuel flow is unchanged.

Why true airspeed is not enough

Range over the ground depends on groundspeed. A strong headwind can substantially reduce distance covered before reaching a fuel limit. That is why flight planning should connect winds aloft, expected groundspeed, route distance and fuel burn instead of treating them as separate problems.

Plan with margins

Real flights include taxi fuel, climb fuel, routing changes, altitude changes, leaning technique, weather deviations and possible delays. Use the aircraft's approved performance information and current operating requirements, then compare actual fuel remaining against the plan during flight.
