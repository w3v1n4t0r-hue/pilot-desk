Aviation speed conversions

Aviation commonly uses knots for airspeed and groundspeed, but manuals, weather products, road-based references and international material may use miles per hour, kilometers per hour or meters per second. Converting correctly matters because a small unit mistake can produce a large planning error.

Knots and nautical miles

One knot means one nautical mile per hour. A nautical mile is based on Earth geometry and is about 1.1508 statute miles or 1.852 kilometers. That direct relationship is why knots pair naturally with aviation distance and time calculations.

Knots to mph

Multiply knots by about 1.1508 to get statute miles per hour. For example, 100 knots is about 115.1 mph. To convert mph back to knots, divide by 1.1508.

Knots to km/h

Multiply knots by 1.852. A speed of 120 knots is about 222 km/h. Divide km/h by 1.852 to return to knots.

Meters per second

One knot is about 0.5144 meters per second. This unit appears in engineering and some meteorological material. Wind forecasts or technical references may use m/s even when cockpit products use knots.

Do not confuse IAS, TAS and groundspeed

A unit conversion changes only the unit. It does not turn indicated airspeed into true airspeed or true airspeed into groundspeed. Those are different physical quantities. Convert the correct speed quantity rather than assuming every number labeled “speed” is interchangeable.

Use a reasonableness check

Because mph is numerically larger than knots for the same speed, 100 knots becoming 87 mph would be an obvious sign that the conversion direction was reversed.

How Aviation speed conversions fits into pilot training

Convert and understand knots, mph, km/h and m/s for aviation, with formulas, examples and common pilot unit mistakes.

Unit conversions are simple only when the starting quantity is identified correctly. Write the unit next to every value, convert one quantity at a time, and perform a reasonableness check before moving the number into another formula. A conversion changes the unit, not the type of speed, distance, pressure, temperature, weight or volume being described.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
