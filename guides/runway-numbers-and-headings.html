Runway numbers and headings

Runway designators give pilots a quick indication of runway direction. In the United States, the number is based on the runway centerline's magnetic azimuth rounded to the nearest ten degrees and expressed without the final zero.

Examples

A runway with a magnetic azimuth near 183° is designated Runway 18. A runway near 87° is designated Runway 9. The FAA notes that an azimuth ending in 5 may round to either adjacent runway number depending on the designation decision.

Reciprocal runway

The opposite end of a runway is approximately 180 degrees away. That means Runway 18 is paired with Runway 36, while Runway 9 is paired with Runway 27.

Parallel runways

Parallel runways with the same magnetic designation use letters such as L, C and R for left, center and right as viewed from the direction of approach.

Runway number is not an exact heading

The painted number is rounded. The actual runway centerline may be several degrees different. The FAA Pilot/Controller Glossary defines runway heading as the magnetic direction corresponding to the runway centerline extended, not simply the runway number multiplied by ten.

Why runway numbers can change

Earth's magnetic field changes over time. If magnetic variation shifts enough, an airport may renumber a runway so the designator remains consistent with the runway's magnetic direction.

Wind comparison

Runway direction is useful when comparing reported wind with the runway for a rough headwind or crosswind picture. For an exact component, use the actual runway direction and wind direction available from current data.
