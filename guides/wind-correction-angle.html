Wind correction angle

Wind correction angle, or WCA, is the angular difference between the desired course over the ground and the heading needed to maintain that course in wind. It is the “crab angle” used to keep the aircraft from drifting sideways.

Why correction is needed

If wind has a crosswind component, the air mass carries the aircraft sideways. Pointing the nose directly along the desired course would allow the ground track to drift downwind.

What changes WCA

A stronger crosswind requires more correction. A slower true airspeed also requires more correction for the same crosswind because the wind is larger relative to the aircraft's speed through the air.

Direction of correction

Correct into the wind. If the wind pushes the aircraft toward the east, the heading must be adjusted toward the west relative to the desired course.

Wind triangle

The full wind-triangle solution combines true course, true airspeed and wind velocity to produce wind correction angle, corrected heading and groundspeed. WCA is therefore one output of the larger vector problem.

Small-angle mental estimate

Pilots often use mental techniques to estimate correction, but the exact relationship depends on wind angle and speed. A calculator or E6B is useful for the planned value, while actual track can be monitored and corrected in flight.

Variation comes afterward

A wind-triangle problem is often solved in true directions first. Magnetic variation is then applied when the result needs to become a magnetic heading. Keep the steps separate to avoid mixing references.

How Wind correction angle fits into pilot training

Learn how wind correction angle keeps an aircraft on course and how wind direction, wind speed and TAS affect the correction.

Navigation problems connect direction, wind, speed, distance, time and position. Keep true and magnetic references consistent, use groundspeed for movement over the earth, and preserve the sign of latitude, longitude and magnetic variation. A useful study check is to estimate the answer before calculating it, then ask whether the result makes sense for the route, wind and time available.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
