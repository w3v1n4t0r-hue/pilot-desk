Glide range formula for pilots

A glide ratio describes how far an aircraft can move forward for each unit of altitude lost under specified conditions. A 9:1 glide ratio means nine units forward for one unit down. That makes it possible to estimate theoretical no-wind glide distance from height above the landing area.

Basic relationship

Horizontal distance is approximately height multiplied by glide ratio, as long as both use the same units. If an aircraft is 5,000 feet above the landing area and has a 9:1 ratio, the geometric distance is about 45,000 feet.

Convert feet to nautical miles

One nautical mile is about 6,076 feet. Dividing 45,000 by 6,076 gives roughly 7.4 NM in still air. That is a geometry estimate, not a guarantee of where the airplane can actually reach.

Wind changes the ground distance

A headwind reduces glide distance over the ground while a tailwind increases it. Wind also changes with altitude, so a single surface wind value may not represent the whole descent. Best-glide speed and configuration must come from the aircraft's approved information.

Height above the landing area matters

Use height above the potential landing site, not simply indicated altitude. If the airplane is at 6,500 feet MSL and the airport elevation is 1,500 feet MSL, the available height is about 5,000 feet before other margins.

Real-world margins

Turns, maneuvering, imperfect speed control, propeller condition, turbulence, sink, terrain and pilot workload can all reduce practical reach. Treat the calculation as a situational-awareness aid and preserve a generous margin.
