CHECKRIDE PREP

Pilot checkride study guides

This hub organizes PilotDesk around the kinds of relationships pilots are commonly expected to understand and explain during training and practical-test preparation. It is not a replacement for the current FAA Airman Certification Standards, handbooks, regulations, instructor guidance or aircraft documents.

Private pilot study topics

Private-pilot preparation usually rewards understanding over memorization. Start with time, speed and distance, crosswind components, fuel planning, pressure altitude, density altitude and weight and balance. The goal is to be able to explain what each number means, which source supplies the input and how you would recognize an unreasonable answer.

Instrument study topics

For instrument work, combine weather interpretation with navigation and descent planning. Review METARs and TAFs, flight categories, 3-degree descent math, holding-leg distance, true and magnetic direction and the navigation reference. PilotDesk calculations are useful as cross-checks; current charts, procedures, weather and ATC information control the real flight.

Commercial pilot study topics

Commercial training adds more emphasis on performance relationships and precise explanation. Useful pages include pivotal altitude, accelerated stalls and load factor, maneuvering speed at weight, turn radius and rate, climb rate versus climb gradient and the broader aircraft performance reference.

Multi-engine checkride topics

Multi-engine study becomes much easier when the topics are connected. Use the multi-engine checkride study guide as the main hub for VMC, VYSE, critical engine, zero sideslip, feathering, engine-out performance, service ceiling and takeoff-performance concepts. Then follow the individual links when you need a deeper explanation.

Emergency and glide-performance questions

Glide questions combine performance math with judgment. The aircraft glide distance calculator shows the basic altitude-times-ratio geometry. Pair it with how far an airplane can glide, best glide speed versus glide ratio and emergency glide planning concepts so the number is not separated from wind, terrain and configuration.

How to study the math for an oral exam

Do not stop at the formula. For every problem, be able to name the inputs, state the units, explain the relationship, estimate the direction the answer should move and identify the approved source you would use for an operational decision. That approach makes the same knowledge useful in the cockpit instead of turning it into a collection of isolated flashcards.

The dedicated pilot checkride math guide organizes the highest-value formulas and links directly to the calculators used to practice them.
