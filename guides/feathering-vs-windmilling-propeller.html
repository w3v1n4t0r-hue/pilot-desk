MULTI-ENGINE AERODYNAMICS

Windmilling Propeller vs Feathered Propeller

If you're comparing a windmilling propeller vs a feathered propeller, the key difference is drag. After a propeller-engine failure, the propeller is not aerodynamically neutral. If the airstream continues to drive the propeller, the rotating system absorbs energy from the airplane and produces substantial drag. Feathering changes blade angle to minimize that drag.

What “windmilling” actually means

A windmilling propeller is being driven by the relative wind rather than by useful engine torque. The airflow must supply the torque required to keep the propeller, reduction gearing if installed, and portions of the engine rotating. That energy comes from the airplane's kinetic and potential energy, so the propeller-engine system acts as a strong aerodynamic brake.

This is why the drag of a windmilling propeller cannot be understood by looking only at the physical frontal area of the blades. The rotating propeller disc is exchanging momentum and energy with a large volume of air.

What feathering changes

Feathering rotates the blades toward a high-pitch orientation that places the blade chord more nearly parallel to the local relative wind. The objective is to reduce the blade angle of attack and the aerodynamic torque that drives windmilling. As rotational drag falls, the failed-engine side of the airplane imposes a much smaller performance penalty.

Feathering does not create thrust, and “feathered” does not mean that every blade is perfectly parallel to every local flow vector. It is a propeller-system state designed to minimize drag after a loss of useful engine power.

Why the failed-engine drag matters so much

In a light twin, available excess power after an engine failure may already be small. Windmilling drag consumes part of that limited performance margin. Reducing propeller drag can therefore change the airplane from a severe descent toward a smaller descent, level flight, or a climb—depending on weight, density altitude, configuration, aircraft condition, and the performance published for that airplane.

Do not interpret that statement as a guarantee of positive climb. The POH/AFM single-engine performance data remain controlling.

Control effect: asymmetric drag adds to asymmetric thrust

An inoperative engine creates an asymmetric-thrust problem because the operating engine continues to produce thrust on one side of the centerline. A windmilling propeller on the failed-engine side also creates asymmetric drag. Both effects increase the yawing moment that the pilot must oppose. Feathering reduces the drag component of that problem.

Why “the propeller looks stopped” is not enough

Visual rotation rate alone does not establish the aerodynamic state of the propeller. The meaningful questions are whether the propeller is producing useful thrust, whether it is windmilling, whether it has reached the feathered blade angle, and what the approved aircraft procedure requires. Cockpit indications and the aircraft system description matter more than visual impression.

Feathering systems are aircraft-specific

The mechanism used to feather and unfeather a propeller depends on the installation. Counterweights, springs, oil pressure, governors, accumulators, start locks, and low-RPM anti-feather devices may be used in different combinations. A generic article should not be used to infer how a particular airplane moves its blades into or out of feather.

For training, know the system installed in the actual airplane and follow its checklist. The aerodynamic reason for feathering is general; the hardware and procedure are not.

Oral-exam answer, stated precisely

If asked why a failed engine's propeller is feathered, a technically complete answer is:

Feathering reduces the aerodynamic torque and drag of the inoperative propeller, which reduces asymmetric drag and preserves as much single-engine performance as the airplane has available.

Technical basis

The FAA Airplane Flying Handbook, Chapter 13, treats loss of directional control and best performance with one engine inoperative as the central multiengine problems. FAA material on propeller feathering likewise describes feathering as a means of minimizing drag after engine failure. See the current Airplane Flying Handbook.
