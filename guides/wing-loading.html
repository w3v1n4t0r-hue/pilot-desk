Aircraft wing loading explained

Wing loading is aircraft weight divided by wing area. It is normally expressed in pounds per square foot in U.S. general aviation. The number tells you how much aircraft weight each square foot of wing area is supporting at a particular operating weight.

Wing loading formula

Wing loading = aircraft weight ÷ wing area. A 2,400-pound airplane with 160 square feet of wing area has a wing loading of 15 lb/ft². If the same airplane weighs 2,000 pounds later in the flight, wing loading drops to 12.5 lb/ft².

Why the ratio changes

Wing area is normally fixed, but aircraft weight changes with fuel, passengers, baggage, and cargo. That means wing loading is not one permanent number unless you are comparing aircraft at a stated reference weight such as maximum takeoff weight.

Wing loading and stall speed

For the same wing and configuration, higher weight requires more lift. That contributes to a higher stall speed at heavier weights. This is why aircraft performance and maneuvering speeds can change with operating weight. Wing loading helps explain the relationship, but the approved POH or AFM remains the source for actual speeds and performance.

Handling and turbulence

Aircraft with relatively high wing loading may feel less responsive to small vertical gusts, while low-wing-loading aircraft can be more easily displaced. That is only a broad design comparison. Wing shape, aspect ratio, control design, speed, mass distribution, and many other factors also affect handling.

Comparing aircraft

Wing loading can be useful when comparing airplanes of similar mission and configuration. A trainer, glider, fighter, and transport aircraft may have dramatically different values because they are designed for different operating speeds and performance goals.
