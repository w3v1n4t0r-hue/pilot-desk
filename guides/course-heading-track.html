Course, heading and track

Course, heading and track sound similar but describe different things. Keeping them separate makes wind-triangle and navigation problems much easier to understand.

Course

Course is the intended path over the ground. A flight planner may begin with a true course between two points and later convert that direction to magnetic course using local variation.

Heading

Heading is the direction the aircraft's nose points. In wind, the heading may need to be offset from the desired course so the aircraft does not drift away from the planned path.

Track

Track is the actual path the aircraft follows over the ground. If the wind correction is perfect, track matches the desired course. If not, track will be displaced.

Wind correction angle

The angle between course and heading that compensates for crosswind is the wind correction angle. The required correction depends on wind direction, wind speed and true airspeed.

True and magnetic references

Course and heading can each be expressed as true or magnetic. Be explicit about the reference. A true course combined with a magnetic heading without applying variation is an inconsistent navigation solution.

Worked concept

If the desired course is north and wind blows from the west, the aircraft may need a heading slightly west of north so the resulting ground track stays northbound. The nose points into the wind while the aircraft moves along the desired course.

Why pilots care

These distinctions appear in VFR navlogs, IFR clearances, GPS displays and ATC instructions. Knowing the term prevents confusion when the aircraft's nose direction and map track do not match.

How Course, heading and track fits into pilot training

Learn the difference between course, heading and track, and how wind correction and magnetic variation connect them in flight planning.

Navigation problems connect direction, wind, speed, distance, time and position. Keep true and magnetic references consistent, use groundspeed for movement over the earth, and preserve the sign of latitude, longitude and magnetic variation. A useful study check is to estimate the answer before calculating it, then ask whether the result makes sense for the route, wind and time available.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
