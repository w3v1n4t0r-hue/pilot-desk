PILOT MATH

Pilot checkride math and formulas

A checkride math question is usually easier when you can explain the relationship before touching a calculator. The important part is knowing what each input represents, keeping units consistent and recognizing when a result does not make sense.

Time, speed and distance

The core relationship is distance = speed × time. Rearranging it gives time = distance ÷ speed and speed = distance ÷ time. Aviation problems usually use nautical miles, knots and hours, so watch the conversion when minutes are involved. Practice with the time, speed and distance calculator and the worked guide.

Wind components and the wind triangle

Crosswind questions test whether you understand the angle between runway direction and the reported wind. Wind-triangle problems add the relationship among true course, true airspeed, wind direction, wind speed, heading and groundspeed. Use the crosswind component calculator and wind triangle calculator to check hand-worked examples.

Fuel, endurance and range

Trip fuel is fuel flow multiplied by time. Endurance is usable fuel divided by fuel flow. Range adds groundspeed to the endurance picture, and reserve requirements must be handled separately using the rule that actually applies to the operation. Review fuel required, endurance and range and the fuel-planning guide.

Pressure altitude and density altitude

Pressure altitude puts the aircraft on the standard-pressure reference. Density altitude adjusts the performance picture for nonstandard atmospheric conditions. The checkride-level concept is not just producing a number; it is explaining why high density altitude reduces aircraft performance. Use the pressure-altitude calculator, density-altitude calculator and current aircraft performance charts.

Climb gradient and descent planning

A climb requirement expressed in feet per nautical mile has to be converted to a vertical speed using groundspeed. Descent planning reverses the same kind of geometry. Review climb gradient to FPM, FPM to climb gradient, 3-degree descent rate and top of descent.

Weight, arm, moment and center of gravity

Moment is weight multiplied by arm. Loaded center of gravity is total moment divided by total weight. The arithmetic is simple, but the operational question depends on the approved loading data, limits and configuration for the aircraft. Use the weight and balance builder, moment and CG calculator and weight-and-balance guide.

Turns, load factor and stall speed

As bank angle increases in a coordinated level turn, load factor increases. Stall speed rises with the square root of load factor. Turn radius and rate also change with true airspeed and bank. Useful practice pages include bank-angle load factor, accelerated stall speed, turn radius and rate of turn.

Glide distance

The simplified still-air relationship is glide distance = height available × glide ratio. A 9:1 ratio with 5,000 feet of usable height gives about 45,000 feet, or roughly 7.4 NM. That does not account for wind, maneuvering, terrain or abnormal configuration. Use the aircraft glide distance calculator and then read how far an airplane can glide so the math stays connected to its assumptions.

Commercial and multi-engine relationships

Commercial preparation commonly adds pivotal altitude, maneuvering speed at weight, power and wing loading, and more detailed aircraft-performance interpretation. Multi-engine preparation adds VMC, critical-engine effects, single-engine climb performance and service ceiling. Those topics are connected in the multi-engine checkride study guide.

A better way to answer an oral math question

State what you are solving for, identify the source and units of each input, estimate the direction and rough size of the answer, do the calculation, and then explain what would change the result. If the number would affect a flight, finish by naming the current approved source that controls the operational decision.
