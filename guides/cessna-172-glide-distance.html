How Far Can a Cessna 172 Glide?

The right answer starts with “which 172?” Use the exact POH for the airplane and treat any generic calculator result as geometry, not guaranteed reach.

There is no honest single glide number for every Cessna 172

“Cessna 172” covers many models, years, weights, propellers, and POH revisions. Best-glide speed can vary with the model and loading, and a generic internet glide ratio should not replace the performance and emergency information in the POH for the airplane you are actually flying.

How to make a useful estimate

  1. Open the exact aircraft POH/AFM and find the applicable best-glide speed or glide-performance information.
  2. Use height above the intended landing surface, not simply indicated altitude MSL.
  3. If the approved data gives or supports a glide ratio for the condition you are studying, multiply usable height by that ratio to estimate still-air horizontal distance.
  4. Reduce your expectations for headwind, turns, terrain, configuration, a windmilling propeller, ice, or anything else that adds drag or consumes altitude.

Example only: if a training problem assumes a 9:1 ratio and 5,000 feet of usable height, the geometry is 45,000 feet, about 7.4 NM in still air. That example is not a published Cessna 172 performance claim.

Why the conservative answer matters

A calculator draws a clean circle; a real forced landing does not. You still need time and altitude to turn, judge the wind, avoid obstacles, configure the airplane, and fly the approach. In an actual emergency, fly the aircraft and follow the current checklist/POH procedure.

Source check

The FAA Airplane Flying Handbook covers emergency approaches and landings. Aircraft-specific speeds and performance must come from the exact Cessna POH/AFM and applicable supplements.
