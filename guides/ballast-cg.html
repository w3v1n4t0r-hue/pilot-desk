Aircraft ballast and center of gravity

Ballast is weight intentionally added to an aircraft to help place the center of gravity within an acceptable range. The amount of weight matters, but its location is equally important because the distance from the aircraft datum determines how much moment the ballast creates.

Weight, arm, and moment

The basic relationship is moment = weight × arm. A 20-pound ballast item at a 100-inch arm creates 2,000 pound-inches of moment. The same 20 pounds at a 50-inch arm creates only 1,000 pound-inches, so the farther location has twice the effect on the aircraft's total moment.

How ballast moves the CG

Adding weight aft of the current CG tends to move the loaded CG aft. Adding weight forward of the current CG tends to move it forward. The exact change depends on current total weight, current total moment, ballast weight, and ballast station.

Solving for ballast weight

If you know current weight and CG, a desired target CG, and the allowable ballast arm, the weight-and-moment equation can be rearranged to solve for the ballast required. PilotDesk's ballast calculator performs that arithmetic, but the target still must fall within the approved envelope.

Why “more ballast” is not always the answer

Adding ballast increases total aircraft weight. That can create a different problem even if it moves CG in the desired direction. Maximum takeoff weight, landing weight, baggage-area limits, floor-loading restrictions, structural limits, and station limits all still apply.

Permanent versus temporary ballast

Aircraft may use permanent ballast installed as part of the approved configuration or temporary/removable ballast under approved procedures. The installation method, attachment, station, and documentation matter; loose weight is not an acceptable substitute for an approved ballast arrangement.

Check the final loading condition

After adding ballast, recalculate total weight and total moment from the beginning and confirm the final point is within the aircraft's approved CG envelope. Also consider how fuel burn may move CG during the flight.

How Aircraft ballast and center of gravity fits into pilot training

Learn how ballast changes aircraft center of gravity, how moment works, and how pilots estimate weight needed to move CG toward a target.

Aircraft-performance math shows how variables are related, but the operational answer remains aircraft specific. Weight, configuration, runway condition, wind, pressure, temperature and technique can all change the result. Use the relationship to understand the trend and to check arithmetic, then use the current POH or AFM procedure and chart for the airplane when the number affects a flight.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
