The 2% True Airspeed Rule

A common training shortcut estimates true airspeed by adding about 2% to calibrated airspeed for each 1,000 ft of altitude. It is useful for quick mental math, but it is not a precision TAS solution.

Rule of thumb

Approx. TAS = CAS × (1 + 0.02 × altitude in thousands of feet)

For example, 110 kt CAS at 8,000 ft gives roughly 128 kt using the shortcut.

Why TAS changes

As air density decreases, an airplane must move faster through the air to produce the same dynamic pressure indicated by the airspeed system. Temperature and actual atmospheric conditions matter, which is why a full TAS calculation or aircraft system can differ from the 2% rule.

Use the right number

Use this calculator for quick estimates and study. For performance, navigation or aircraft-specific operating decisions, use the appropriate approved data and accurate atmospheric inputs.

Worked 2 percent rule example

With 110 knots calibrated airspeed at 8,000 feet, the training shortcut adds about 16 percent: 110 × 1.16 is about 128 knots true airspeed. This is only a quick estimate. Nonstandard temperature and compressibility can make the real value different, especially as altitude and speed increase.

CAS, TAS, and groundspeed are different

Calibrated airspeed corrects indicated airspeed for instrument and position error. True airspeed corrects for air density. Groundspeed is the airplane's speed over the ground after wind is accounted for. Mixing those three values is a common source of bad time, wind, and turn calculations.

How The 2% True Airspeed Rule fits into pilot training

Learn the common true-airspeed 2-percent-per-thousand-feet training estimate and when not to use it as precision performance data.

Aircraft-performance math shows how variables are related, but the operational answer remains aircraft specific. Weight, configuration, runway condition, wind, pressure, temperature and technique can all change the result. Use the relationship to understand the trend and to check arithmetic, then use the current POH or AFM procedure and chart for the airplane when the number affects a flight.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
