Feathering vs Windmilling Propeller

A failed engine is only part of the engine-out problem. The propeller attached to that engine can create a huge amount of drag if it continues to windmill. Understanding the difference between a windmilling propeller and a feathered propeller is central to multi-engine performance.

What windmilling means

A windmilling propeller is being driven by the airflow rather than by engine power. The rotating blades extract energy from the airstream and produce drag. On a light twin, that drag can be severe enough to destroy much of the remaining single-engine climb capability.

What feathering does

Feathering rotates the propeller blades toward an angle that presents a much smaller frontal area to the relative wind. The propeller slows or stops, and drag drops dramatically. The exact feathering system varies by aircraft and propeller installation.

Why this matters for control

Drag from the failed-engine side increases the yawing tendency in addition to reducing climb performance. Reducing that drag makes the airplane easier to control and improves the amount of performance available from the operating engine.

Feathering is aircraft-specific

Some airplanes use oil pressure, springs, counterweights, accumulators or other system features to move the blades toward or away from feather. Training twins can also include devices that delay feathering at low RPM to reduce the chance of accidentally feathering during normal ground operations. Always use the current aircraft system description and checklist.

How this connects to the rest of multi-engine training
