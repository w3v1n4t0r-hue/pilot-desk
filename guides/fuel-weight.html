GUIDE

Aviation fuel weight

Fuel is measured by volume at the pump but enters aircraft loading calculations as weight. Converting gallons or liters to pounds or kilograms is therefore a normal part of flight planning and weight-and-balance work.

Why the conversion is approximate

Fuel density varies with fuel type and temperature. Training references often use convenient planning factors, but the aircraft's approved documentation and the fuel supplier's information are better sources when precision matters.

Common planning factors

For rough general-aviation planning, avgas is often treated as about 6 pounds per U.S. gallon and Jet A as roughly 6.7 pounds per U.S. gallon. These are estimates rather than universal constants.

Worked example

Using the common 6 lb/gal avgas shortcut, 40 U.S. gallons would be estimated at about 240 pounds. That weight must be included in total aircraft weight and its moment must be calculated using the applicable fuel-tank arm or approved loading method.

Usable versus total fuel

Do not assume all fuel listed in tank capacity is usable. Weight-and-balance records and POH/AFM data distinguish usable fuel from unusable fuel, and the empty-weight definition may already account for unusable fuel depending on the aircraft documentation.

Fuel burn changes aircraft weight

As fuel is consumed, total weight decreases. Depending on tank location and fuel system design, center of gravity can move as well. A loading condition that is acceptable at departure should also be checked for later phases when the approved envelope or procedure requires it.

Keep units consistent

If the rest of a loading worksheet uses pounds and inches, do not mix kilograms or liters into the moment math without converting them first. Unit consistency prevents a large class of avoidable errors.
