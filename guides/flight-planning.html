PILOT PLANNING GUIDE

A Practical Flight Planning Workflow

Good flight planning is not one calculation. It is a sequence of checks that should agree with one another: the airplane you are actually flying, the route, current airport information, weather, fuel and performance, procedures, airspace, and any operational restrictions that apply.

Training reference only. This workflow does not replace regulations, company procedures, an official weather briefing, a filed flight plan, or current approved aircraft information.

1. Start with the aircraft you are actually flying

Before working on the route, confirm the aircraft configuration and current source data that matter to the flight. Typical planning inputs include current empty weight and balance information, fuel capacity, expected fuel burn, cruise speed, applicable performance charts, and any limitations that affect the route or runway. A generic model value can be useful for practice but should not silently replace the data applicable to a specific airplane.

2. Define departure, destination, and likely route

Start with the airports and then build the route appropriate to the operation. For a training flight this may be direct or a set of visual checkpoints. For an IFR flight it may involve published routes, fixes, departures, arrivals, or expected ATC routing. Plotting the route on current chart information helps reveal airspace, terrain, special-use airspace, and route geometry that a text list can hide.

3. Check the airports early

Review runway layout, field elevation, frequencies, airport notes, and current operational information before spending too much time optimizing a route. A runway closure, construction NOTAM, lighting issue, or weather problem can change the plan immediately. PilotDesk's Airport Search is a starting point, but published FAA airport information and current NOTAM sources remain authoritative.

4. Build the weather picture, not just a METAR

Surface observations are only one layer. Compare METARs with TAFs and broader forecast products, then consider winds, ceilings, visibility, precipitation, convection, icing, turbulence, fronts, and other hazards relevant to the flight. Also look at trends: a single good observation does not mean conditions will remain good for the entire flight.

5. Check NOTAMs, TFRs, and airspace

Weather can be excellent while the route is still unusable. Review current NOTAMs, temporary flight restrictions, airport/runway status, special-use airspace, and any other restrictions that apply. Recheck time-sensitive items close to departure because they can change after the first planning pass.

6. Calculate navigation and time

Use route distance, course, forecast wind, and true airspeed to estimate wind correction, groundspeed, time enroute, and headings. Keep the source of the wind clear. Surface airport wind should not be silently substituted for an enroute wind forecast. PilotDesk's Route Planner deliberately separates the user-entered planning wind from the endpoint METAR context.

7. Calculate fuel with the required reserve and real margins

Trip fuel is not the entire fuel plan. Include start, taxi, climb, cruise, descent, expected delays, reserve requirements, and any additional margin appropriate to the operation. Then compare the result with usable fuel, weight limits, and the expected landing condition. If a small change in wind or routing destroys the margin, that is useful information before departure.

8. Check weight, balance, and performance

Verify the airplane remains within applicable weight and CG limits for the relevant phases of flight. Then use the approved performance information for the actual conditions and configuration. Runway length by itself is not a performance calculation; pressure altitude, temperature, wind, runway condition, slope, weight, obstacle requirements, and aircraft configuration can all matter.

9. Review applicable procedures

For IFR operations, review the current procedures that may be used at the departure, destination, and alternates. For VFR operations, airport diagrams and published airport information can still prevent confusion. PilotDesk links current FAA d-TPP documents without replacing the original FAA source.

10. Do a final cross-check

Near departure, revisit the items most likely to have changed: weather, NOTAMs, TFRs, runway status, fuel, and routing. Make sure the final plan still matches the airplane and the operation. A planning tool is most useful when it helps you notice disagreement between sources, not when it makes the plan look more certain than it is.
