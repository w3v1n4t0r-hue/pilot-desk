Density Altitude in Aviation: Formula, Meaning & Performance

If you're searching for what density altitude means in aviation or how to calculate density altitude, start here. Density altitude is not the airplane's geometric altitude. It is the altitude in the standard atmosphere at which the air would have the same density as the air currently surrounding the airplane. That definition is useful because aircraft performance depends strongly on air density.

Common training approximation:
DA ≈ PA + 120 × (OAT − TISA)

The equation above is a rule of thumb, not the definition of density altitude and not an exact atmosphere model. It is useful because it exposes the two dominant pilot inputs: pressure altitude and temperature deviation from standard.

Start with pressure altitude

Pressure altitude is the altitude in the standard atmosphere corresponding to the existing atmospheric pressure. Setting an altimeter to 29.92 inHg provides pressure altitude directly from the instrument, subject to instrument/system error. A common mental estimate is:

PA ≈ Field elevation + (29.92 − altimeter setting) × 1,000 ft

The 1,000-feet-per-inch factor is approximate. For aircraft-performance work, use the method required by the POH/AFM or performance chart.

Then compare actual temperature with ISA

In the International Standard Atmosphere troposphere, temperature decreases from 15°C at mean sea level at approximately 1.98°C per 1,000 feet. Pilots usually round that to 2°C per 1,000 feet for mental math.

ISA temperature ≈ 15°C − 2°C per 1,000 ft of pressure altitude

The temperature difference OAT − ISA temperature is the ISA deviation. Warmer-than-standard air is less dense than standard air at the same pressure altitude, so density altitude increases.

Why the 120 ft/°C rule works only approximately

The familiar 120 ft/°C relationship linearizes a nonlinear atmosphere over the range commonly encountered in light-aircraft training. It is excellent for a reasonableness check and quick cockpit estimate. It is not a replacement for an atmosphere model, a manufacturer performance chart, or an approved flight-planning method.

PilotDesk's density-altitude calculator uses a dry-air standard-atmosphere model and converts the calculated density back to an ISA-equivalent altitude. Because that method and the 120 ft/°C shortcut are not mathematically identical, small differences are expected.

Worked example

Assume a field elevation of 5,000 ft, altimeter setting 30.12 inHg, and OAT 30°C.

  1. Approximate pressure altitude: 5,000 + (29.92 − 30.12) × 1,000 = 4,800 ft.
  2. Approximate ISA temperature at 4,800 ft: about 5°C.
  3. ISA deviation: 30 − 5 = +25°C.
  4. Quick density-altitude estimate: 4,800 + 120 × 25 = 7,800 ft.

The airport remains physically near 5,000 ft MSL. The 7,800-ft value describes the density of the air in standard-atmosphere terms.

What high density altitude does to performance

Low density changes several parts of the aircraft system at once. A normally aspirated engine ingests less oxygen mass per intake cycle and therefore develops less power. A propeller operates in less dense air and produces less thrust for a given geometric and rotational condition. The wing requires a higher true airspeed to generate the same dynamic pressure and indicated airspeed.

The practical result is usually a longer takeoff roll, poorer climb performance, and higher true airspeed for a given indicated airspeed. The exact magnitude is aircraft-specific and must come from the current POH/AFM performance data.

A precision point: density altitude does not mean “the wing makes less lift at the same IAS”

That phrase is too crude. In subsonic flight, indicated airspeed is fundamentally a dynamic-pressure measurement. At the same indicated airspeed, configuration, and angle-of-attack relationship, the wing does not simply lose lift because density altitude is higher. What changes is the true airspeed required to produce that indicated airspeed, along with engine and propeller performance. Those effects are what make takeoff and climb performance deteriorate.

Humidity

Moist air is less dense than dry air at the same temperature and pressure because water-vapor molecules have lower molecular mass than the nitrogen-oxygen mixture they replace. Humidity can therefore increase the effective density-altitude penalty. Many simple training formulas and many dry-air atmosphere calculators omit humidity, so the model being used should be stated explicitly.

Why two density-altitude calculators may disagree

Before assuming one tool is wrong, compare the methods. Differences can come from:

  • using the 120 ft/°C approximation versus a standard-atmosphere density solution;
  • rounding pressure altitude or ISA temperature at different steps;
  • different standard-atmosphere constants;
  • including or excluding humidity;
  • using field elevation where pressure altitude was required.

What density altitude does not tell you

Density altitude alone is not takeoff distance, climb rate, accelerate-stop distance, or obstacle-clearance capability. Those outputs depend on aircraft weight, configuration, runway condition, wind, slope, engine/propeller condition, and the performance method published for the airplane.

Technical basis

The FAA Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge defines pressure altitude using the standard datum plane and describes density altitude as the altitude in the standard atmosphere corresponding to a given density. It also identifies high elevation, low pressure, high temperature, and high humidity as conditions associated with high density altitude. See the current Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge.
