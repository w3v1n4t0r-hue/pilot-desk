Zero Sideslip in Multi-Engine Airplanes

After an engine failure, the airplane is exposed to asymmetric thrust and drag. Multi-engine training teaches pilots to use rudder to control yaw and a small bank toward the operating engine to reduce sideslip and improve control and performance.

Training reference only. Use the current POH/AFM and your instructor's procedure for the airplane you fly.

What zero sideslip means

Zero sideslip is the condition where the airplane is aligned so the relative wind is not crossing the fuselage sideways. In an engine-out condition, that normally requires coordinated rudder and a slight bank toward the operating engine rather than simply centering the inclinometer ball.

Why the bank helps

The operating engine creates yaw toward the failed side. Rudder opposes that yaw. A slight bank toward the operating engine creates a horizontal lift component that helps balance the side force from the rudder, reducing drag and improving the airplane's ability to maintain control.

Zero sideslip and VMC

Bank toward the operating engine is also one of the conditions that affects minimum control speed. Removing the favorable bank makes directional control more difficult and tends to increase VMC. See the VMC factors guide for the full relationship.

Zero sideslip and climb performance

Excess sideslip adds drag at exactly the time a twin has already lost a large portion of its available climb performance. That is why multi-engine instructors emphasize correct rudder pressure, the appropriate bank, configuration cleanup, and the published single-engine climb speed.

Common oral-exam question

Why isn't the ball necessarily centered during zero-sideslip single-engine flight? Because the inclinometer responds to lateral acceleration, not directly to aerodynamic sideslip. In many twins, the correct zero-sideslip condition shows the ball slightly displaced toward the operating engine.
