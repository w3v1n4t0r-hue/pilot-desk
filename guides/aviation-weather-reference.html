Aviation weather reference

A weather briefing is much more than one METAR or a colored map dot. This reference organizes PilotDesk's weather guides around observations, forecasts and common interpretation concepts while linking back to current weather tools.

METAR and TAF fundamentals

How to read METARs and TAFs provides the broad overview. PilotDesk's METAR decoder breaks a report into readable groups.

Wind and gusts

METAR wind and gusts explains direction, sustained speed, gusts, variable wind and the important distinction between true METAR wind direction and magnetic runway references.

Ceiling and visibility

METAR ceiling and visibility explains BKN, OVC, vertical visibility and prevailing visibility. Flight categories covers the commonly displayed VFR, MVFR, IFR and LIFR thresholds.

Forecast changes

TAF change groups explains forecast transitions such as FM, TEMPO and probability groups where used. Times are read in UTC, so date boundaries deserve careful attention.

Temperature, dew point and clouds

Temperature-dew point spread explains what a narrowing spread can suggest about saturation. Cloud-base estimation explains a training shortcut and why it cannot replace an observed ceiling.

Weather and aircraft performance

Density altitude shows how temperature, pressure and elevation affect aircraft performance. Weather that appears visually benign can still create meaningful performance penalties on a hot, high day.

Use the complete picture

PilotDesk's weather page is a supplemental display. Actual flight decisions should include current official observations, forecasts, NOTAMs, TFRs and other required information appropriate to the operation.

How Aviation weather reference fits into pilot training

A pilot weather reference for METARs, TAFs, flight categories, wind, ceiling, visibility, dew point and cloud-base concepts.

Weather knowledge becomes useful when the observation or forecast is connected to a specific flight decision. Read the reported values in context, keep observation time and forecast period in mind, and separate what the weather product actually says from what a rule of thumb only estimates. When aircraft performance is affected, carry the current atmospheric values into the approved performance method rather than stopping at the weather interpretation.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
