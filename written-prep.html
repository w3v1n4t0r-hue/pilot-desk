200 ft/NM at 90 knots

A departure requires 200 ft/NM. At 90 kt groundspeed, what vertical speed matches that gradient?

  1. Convert speed to distance per minute: 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5 NM/min.
  2. Multiply by the required gradient: 200 × 1.5 = 300 fpm.
  3. Check the units: ft/NM × NM/min leaves ft/min.

Why 200 fpm is wrong: the published number is per nautical mile, not per minute. Why 400 fpm is a different case: it matches 200 ft/NM at 120 kt groundspeed. Use groundspeed, not indicated airspeed, for this conversion.

A correct conversion does not prove the airplane can meet the departure requirement. Compare it with current aircraft performance at the planned weight, pressure altitude and temperature, and follow the published procedure.

Work another case in the climb-gradient calculator, then read the gradient explanation. For departure context, consult the FAA Instrument Procedures Handbook, Chapter 1.

Moving a bag changes balance, even when weight stays the same

A 2,400 lb airplane has a 50 lb bag moved 18 inches aft. Nothing is added or removed.

  1. Find the change in moment: 50 lb × 18 in = 900 lb-in.
  2. Divide by total weight: 900 ÷ 2,400 = 0.375 inch aft.
  3. Add that change to the previous CG, then compare the new value with the approved envelope at the loaded weight.

The bag moves 18 inches, but it is only a small fraction of the airplane’s weight. The whole airplane’s CG therefore moves much less. Being below maximum weight does not establish acceptable balance; verify the CG and baggage-compartment limits too.

Use the weight-and-balance calculator with your actual aircraft data, and check the FAA Pilot’s Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, Chapter 10.

What this question bank covers

PilotDesk publishes 224 practice questions: Private (53), Instrument (28), Commercial (27), CFI (30), CFII (28), ATP (29), and Fundamentals of Instruction (29). Each track is a small selection for practice, not complete coverage of every FAA knowledge-test objective. These are seven separate tracks; choosing one does not give you all 224 questions.

Pilot tracks use FAA ACS or PTS references where applicable. FOI questions use Aviation Instructor’s Handbook chapter references for subjects such as learning, communication, assessment and instructor responsibilities. Those chapter references are not official FAA test-report codes.

Private Pilot: work through nine lessons and subject practice →

Choose a topic to work on

  • Private: calculate baggage shifts, separate a METAR ceiling from scattered clouds, solve a wind triangle and budget fuel reserve.
  • Instrument: interpret cold-temperature altitude error, DME slant range, compass errors and departure climb-rate requirements.
  • Commercial: connect bank angle to stall speed, compare touchdown energy, calculate pivotal altitude and explain a conventional twin’s critical engine.
  • CFI: diagnose a learner’s errors in night approaches, energy management, maneuvering limits, navigation references and loaded CG.
  • CFII: teach unit checks, departure capability, alternate-static indications and the geometry behind DME.
  • ATP: relate Mach to temperature, distinguish engine rpm from thrust, and evaluate speed, mass and convective-weather hazards.
  • FOI: design useful distractors, identify lesson conditions, assess course breadth and check transfer beyond memorized answers.

Original practice scenarios identify the relevant FAA handbook, standard or AIM section and explain each answer choice. The bank has not received independent CFI sign-off; source checks are editorial verification.

Reviewing missed questions

Choose one topic and answer without looking up the solution. Read the explanation for every choice, open the source, and write down the rule or relationship you missed. Return to missed questions after studying that subject. Finally, use a mixed session to check whether you can recognize the same idea without a topic cue.

Exam mode mixes topics in the selected track and uses a timer scaled to the available practice questions. It does not reproduce the complete FAA exam or predict a passing score. Bring a complete study resource and the current FAA test guidance into your preparation.

The question bank contains PilotDesk-authored questions and identified FAA sample material; it is not the FAA’s live exam bank or a copy of a commercial provider’s bank. See our editorial and corrections policy or report a question that needs correction.
