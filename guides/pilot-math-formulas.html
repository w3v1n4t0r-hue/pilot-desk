Time, speed, and distance

The basic relationship is distance = groundspeed × time. Rearranging it gives time = distance ÷ groundspeed and groundspeed = distance ÷ time. Keep the units consistent: knots are nautical miles per hour, so 120 knots covers 2 NM each minute. Use the time, speed, distance calculator or the NM per minute calculator for quick checks, then use the time-speed-distance guide if you want a worked explanation.

Fuel required, endurance, and range

Trip fuel is fuel-burn rate multiplied by trip time. Reserve fuel uses the same relationship after reserve minutes are converted to hours. Endurance is usable fuel divided by fuel burn, while still-air range is endurance multiplied by groundspeed. Real flight planning can require separate start, taxi, climb, alternate, holding, contingency, and regulatory fuel. Use fuel required, endurance and range, and fuel weight, then read the fuel planning guide or endurance and range guide.

Crosswind and headwind components

If θ is the angle between the wind direction and the runway heading, crosswind is wind speed × sin(θ), and the along-runway component is wind speed × cos(θ). A negative along-runway answer represents tailwind. Keep wind and runway directions on the same true or magnetic reference. Runway headings are magnetic, tower-issued winds are magnetic, and transmitted surface observations are referenced to true north. Use the crosswind component calculator and the crosswind guide.

Wind correction angle and groundspeed

A wind triangle resolves the wind into cross-course and along-course components. The cross-course component determines wind correction angle; the along-course component changes groundspeed. The result connects directly to time and fuel planning, because groundspeed changes both. Use the wind triangle calculator, wind triangle guide, and wind-correction-angle guide. For true/magnetic direction work, use the true/magnetic heading calculator.

Pressure altitude

A common quick estimate is field elevation + (29.92 − altimeter setting) × 1,000 feet. Lower-than-standard pressure raises pressure altitude; higher pressure lowers it. Use the pressure altitude calculator and pressure altitude guide.

Density altitude and ISA

Density altitude expresses the altitude in the standard atmosphere that has the same density as the current air. Temperature, pressure, and elevation all matter. PilotDesk uses an ISA atmosphere model rather than treating the classroom 120-feet-per-degree shortcut as a precision solution. Use the density altitude calculator, ISA temperature calculator, density altitude guide, and ISA temperature guide.

Climb gradient and vertical speed

Vertical speed in feet per minute equals required climb gradient in ft/NM multiplied by groundspeed in NM/min. The reverse calculation divides vertical speed by groundspeed. Because groundspeed changes with wind, the vertical speed needed to satisfy a fixed gradient changes too. Use climb gradient to FPM, FPM to climb gradient, gradient/angle conversion, and the climb gradient guide.

Three-degree descent and top of descent

For an exact 3° path, vertical speed is groundspeed × 6,076.1 ÷ 60 × tan(3°). The familiar mental shortcut is groundspeed × 5. Descent distance can be estimated as altitude to lose divided by the planned descent gradient. Use the 3° descent rate calculator, top of descent calculator, and the related descent and TOD guides.

Turns, load factor, and accelerated stall speed

In a coordinated level turn, load factor is 1 ÷ cos(bank angle). The theoretical accelerated stall speed is the 1-G stall speed multiplied by the square root of load factor. Turn radius and rate also depend on true airspeed and bank angle. Use the load factor, accelerated stall, turn radius, rate of turn, and standard-rate bank calculators. The turn radius/rate guide ties the relationships together.

Pivotal altitude

The common feet-and-knots training approximation is pivotal altitude AGL ≈ groundspeed² ÷ 11.3. Because groundspeed is squared, the answer changes quickly as speed changes. Use the pivotal altitude calculator and pivotal altitude guide.

Weight, moment, CG, ballast, and percent MAC

Moment is weight × arm. Loaded center of gravity is total moment ÷ total weight. Percent MAC is (CG − LEMAC) ÷ MAC length × 100. These equations do the arithmetic, but the approved aircraft envelope determines whether the loading is acceptable. Use the moment and CG calculator, percent MAC calculator, ballast calculator, and weight and balance builder. Continue with the weight-and-balance guide, envelope guide, or percent-MAC guide.

Navigation formulas

For navigation math, PilotDesk includes great-circle distance, DME arc distance, reciprocal heading, true/magnetic heading, DMS/decimal coordinates, and holding-leg distance. The navigation reference hub groups the corresponding explanations together.

Aviation conversions and performance formulas

Common conversion work includes speed, distance, temperature, pressure, weight, volume, and vertical speed. Performance-related formulas include Mach number, speed of sound, dynamic hydroplaning speed, wing loading, and power loading. Browse the aviation conversion hub or aircraft performance reference for the grouped explanations.

How to use pilot math well

Use a formula to understand the relationship, a calculator to reduce arithmetic errors, and the current approved source to make an operational decision. A result should also pass a reasonableness check. If a 20-knot wind at 30° produces a 30-knot crosswind, or 120 knots somehow covers 10 NM per minute, stop and check the units before doing anything with the answer.
