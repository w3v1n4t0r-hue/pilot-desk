Aviation fuel weight explained

Pilots often need to convert gallons of fuel into pounds before completing a weight-and-balance calculation. The basic relationship is simple: fuel weight equals fuel volume multiplied by the planning density used for that fuel.

Common planning values

A common training approximation for 100LL avgas is about 6 pounds per U.S. gallon. Jet A is commonly estimated near 6.7 pounds per U.S. gallon. These are planning values, not universal physical constants. Actual density changes with fuel type and temperature, so use aircraft and operator data when precision matters.

Worked avgas example

If an airplane carries 40 gallons of avgas and you use 6 lb/gal, the estimated fuel weight is 240 pounds. If 15 gallons are burned during a flight, that is about 90 pounds removed from the airplane.

Why fuel weight matters

Fuel can represent a large part of a light airplane's useful load. Adding full fuel may reduce the passenger or baggage load available before reaching maximum takeoff weight. Because fuel tanks also have a specific arm, burning fuel can move center of gravity as well as reduce total weight.

Gallons are not pounds

One of the easiest mistakes is entering fuel volume directly into a weight field. A weight-and-balance worksheet needs weight, usually pounds or kilograms, while fuel gauges and receipts often show gallons or liters. Convert first, then use the correct tank arm or moment data from the approved aircraft information.

Cross-check the result

Forty gallons of avgas should be in the neighborhood of 240 pounds, not 40 pounds or 2,400 pounds. Simple reasonableness checks are valuable before relying on any calculator output.
