Climb rate versus climb gradient

Climb rate and climb gradient both describe climb performance, but they measure different things. Confusing them can make an obstacle-clearance calculation look better or worse than it really is.

Climb rate

Climb rate is vertical speed, usually expressed in feet per minute. If the airplane gains 800 feet each minute, the climb rate is 800 FPM.

Climb gradient

Climb gradient describes altitude gained per horizontal distance, commonly feet per nautical mile or percent gradient. It tells you how steeply the aircraft climbs over the ground.

Groundspeed connects them

For a given climb gradient, a higher groundspeed requires a higher FPM because the aircraft covers more horizontal distance each minute. A lower groundspeed requires less FPM for the same ft/NM gradient.

Worked example

If a required gradient is 300 ft/NM and groundspeed is 120 knots, the airplane travels 2 NM per minute. Required climb rate is therefore about 600 FPM.

Headwind and tailwind

A headwind lowers groundspeed and can improve climb gradient over the ground even if the aircraft's aerodynamic climb rate is unchanged. A tailwind increases groundspeed and can reduce the achieved ft/NM gradient.

Aircraft performance still controls

The calculator converts between quantities; it does not prove the airplane can achieve the needed climb. Compare the requirement to approved climb-performance data under the actual weight, altitude, temperature and configuration.
