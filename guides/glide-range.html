GUIDE

Glide range and glide ratio

A glide ratio describes the horizontal distance an aircraft can theoretically travel for each unit of altitude lost in still air at the appropriate glide condition. It is useful for understanding geometry, but actual emergency glide performance depends on much more than a ratio.

What a 9:1 glide ratio means

A 9:1 ratio means about nine units forward for one unit down. The units can be feet, meters or another consistent distance unit. A 9:1 aircraft at 5,000 feet above the landing surface therefore has a simple geometric range of about 45,000 feet in still air before other factors are considered.

Convert to nautical miles

After calculating horizontal distance in feet, divide by about 6,076 feet per nautical mile. In the example above, 45,000 feet is roughly 7.4 NM.

Altitude above the landing surface matters

Use height available to lose, not simply indicated altitude. If terrain or the landing site is already elevated, subtract that elevation to estimate usable height.

Wind changes ground range

A headwind reduces how far the aircraft travels over the ground while descending. A tailwind increases ground range. The best glide airspeed may also be adjusted by approved guidance for weight or wind, depending on the aircraft.

Turns and configuration cost energy

Bank angle, maneuvering, propeller condition, landing gear, flaps, damage, engine windmilling and pilot technique can all reduce the simple geometric range. The straight-line calculation should not be treated as a guaranteed reachable circle.

Use the POH or AFM

The aircraft's approved emergency procedures and glide-performance information are the correct operational reference. A generic ratio calculator is useful for training and visualization only.
