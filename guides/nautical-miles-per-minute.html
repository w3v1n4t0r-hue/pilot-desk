Nautical miles per minute

Converting groundspeed from knots into nautical miles per minute is one of the quickest pieces of mental math a pilot can use. It makes ETA estimates, holding distance, top-of-descent checks and position updates much easier.

The basic conversion

Knots are nautical miles per hour, so divide groundspeed by 60 to get nautical miles per minute. At 120 knots, the airplane covers 2 NM each minute. At 90 knots, it covers 1.5 NM per minute.

Seconds per nautical mile

Another useful form is seconds per NM. Divide 3,600 by groundspeed in knots. At 120 knots, one NM takes about 30 seconds. At 180 knots, one NM takes about 20 seconds.

Quick ETA example

If 24 NM remain and groundspeed is 120 knots, the airplane is covering 2 NM per minute, so the time remaining is about 12 minutes.

Why groundspeed matters

Use groundspeed for distance-over-ground timing. True airspeed does not include the wind effect and can produce the wrong ETA when a meaningful headwind or tailwind exists.

Holding and procedure math

If groundspeed is 90 knots, a one-minute leg covers roughly 1.5 NM in still conditions. Actual holding tracks are affected by wind correction, turn geometry and procedure requirements, so the simple calculation is only a planning estimate.

Descent planning

NM per minute helps connect altitude to distance. If a descent must lose 3,000 feet over 10 NM and groundspeed is known, you can quickly estimate both the time available and the vertical speed required.

How Nautical miles per minute fits into pilot training

Learn how groundspeed converts to nautical miles per minute and seconds per NM for quick ETA, descent and holding checks.

Navigation problems connect direction, wind, speed, distance, time and position. Keep true and magnetic references consistent, use groundspeed for movement over the earth, and preserve the sign of latitude, longitude and magnetic variation. A useful study check is to estimate the answer before calculating it, then ask whether the result makes sense for the route, wind and time available.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
