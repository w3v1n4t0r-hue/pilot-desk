MULTI-ENGINE

Identify, Verify, Feather

“Identify, verify, feather” is a training framework for dealing with an engine failure in a multi-engine airplane. The exact actions, order and limitations are airplane-specific, so the approved checklist and instructor procedures always control. The framework is useful because it separates three different decisions that should not be rushed together.

Training concept only. Do not use this page as an emergency checklist. Follow the current POH/AFM and approved aircraft checklist.

Identify the failed engine

An engine failure usually creates yaw toward the failed side, roll tendencies, loss of performance and changes in engine indications. A classic memory aid is “dead foot, dead engine”: the foot not requiring rudder pressure is associated with the failed side. That is only an identification cue, not permission to secure an engine.

Verify before securing

Verification exists to prevent shutting down the operating engine. In training, pilots confirm that manipulating the suspected engine's control produces the expected response before committing to a shutdown. The specific control movement and exact sequence must match the airplane's approved procedure.

Why feathering matters

A windmilling propeller can create enormous drag. Feathering turns the blades toward a low-drag angle, reducing the aerodynamic penalty from the failed engine. That can make a major difference in single-engine climb capability and aircraft controllability.

Control first, then troubleshoot

The broader lesson is priorities. Maintain directional control, pitch for the appropriate aircraft-specific speed, configure the airplane as required, identify the problem, verify it, and then accomplish the approved procedure. Multi-engine training emphasizes that an incorrect action on the operating engine can turn a manageable failure into a total power loss.
