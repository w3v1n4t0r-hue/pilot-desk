How to Read METARs and TAFs

METARs tell you what a reporting station observed. TAFs describe forecast conditions for a terminal area over a stated valid period. Both use compact coded groups so a pilot can scan a lot of information quickly, but the code only becomes useful when you keep the report time, forecast period, and larger weather picture in mind.

Training reference only. Use current official aviation weather sources and an appropriate briefing for operational decisions.

Start with the station and time

A report such as METAR KMCO 101553Z identifies KMCO and an observation on the 10th day of the month at 15:53 UTC. TAFs also include an issue time and a valid period. When comparing reports, check the time before comparing the weather itself; a perfectly decoded report can still be too old to describe what is happening now.

Wind

A wind group such as 19010G18KT means wind from 190 degrees true at 10 knots with gusts to 18 knots. VRB06KT means direction variable at 6 knots. Some reports add a variable-direction group such as 160V230. Surface wind is airport context and should not be substituted for an enroute wind forecast.

Visibility

In U.S. METARs, prevailing visibility is commonly shown in statute miles. A group such as 10SM means 10 statute miles. Lower values can be written as whole numbers, fractions, or mixed numbers. Runway visual range may appear in a separate RVR group when applicable.

Weather phenomena

Weather groups combine intensity, descriptors, and phenomena. RA means rain, SN snow, BR mist, FG fog, TS thunderstorm, SH showers, and FZ freezing. A minus sign means light and a plus sign means heavy for applicable phenomena. Because several codes can be combined, always keep the original group visible while interpreting it.

Clouds and ceilings

Cloud groups use FEW, SCT, BKN, OVC, or VV followed by height in hundreds of feet AGL. BKN020 means broken clouds at 2,000 feet AGL. In aviation weather reporting, broken and overcast layers and vertical visibility can establish a ceiling; few and scattered layers do not by themselves.

Temperature and dew point

A group such as 22/12 means temperature 22°C and dew point 12°C. A leading M indicates a negative value. The temperature/dew-point spread can help you think about moisture and possible visibility or cloud concerns, but it is only one part of the weather picture.

Altimeter setting

In a U.S. METAR, A2989 means an altimeter setting of 29.89 inHg. Other regions may use a QNH group such as Q1013, expressed in hectopascals.

TAF base conditions and change groups

A TAF begins with forecast conditions that apply until a change group modifies them. FM introduces conditions expected from a specific time forward. TEMPO identifies temporary fluctuations during a stated period. Other permitted groups can describe gradual changes or probabilities depending on the issuing format and region. The safest habit is to read the TAF as a timeline rather than as one long sentence.

Compare the METAR to the TAF

Once you understand each report, compare the current observation with what the TAF expected. If the METAR is already worse than forecast, or weather is changing faster than the TAF timing implies, that is a reason to widen your weather review rather than simply trusting the forecast line you first noticed.
