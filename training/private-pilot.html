Pilot qualifications and privileges

ACS study group: PA.I.A

Separate the certificate you hold, medical eligibility, flight-review status and recent experience for the operation. A current review does not prove night passenger currency, and being current does not establish proficiency in today’s conditions.

Try this case

You and one passenger share an otherwise permissible common-purpose flight. Allowable expenses total $180. What is your minimum share, and what does paying that share fail to prove?

Show the explanation

Two people split the allowable expenses, so your minimum share is $90. The arithmetic does not establish that the proposed flight meets all compensation and common-purpose requirements. Separately check privileges, medical eligibility, review status and the applicable recent experience.

Watch for: A paid fuel bill is not a flight-review endorsement. Recent daytime landings do not establish the separate night passenger requirement.

Airworthiness and inoperative equipment

ACS study group: PA.I.B

Use maintenance records, the airplane’s required equipment and its approved operating information together. An inspection entry answers one question; it does not establish that every installed item is serviceable or that a new discrepancy may be deferred.

Try this case

A normally certificated training airplane is eligible for the no-MEL process. Its landing light is broken before a proposed daytime flight. Is placing an “Inoperative” label beside the switch enough to dispatch?

Show the explanation

No. Determine whether the item is required by the relevant certification and equipment documents, operating rules or an AD. If deferral is permitted, complete the required removal or deactivation, placarding and applicable maintenance records. An appropriately qualified person must also determine that the failure is not a hazard. The daytime label alone does not answer those checks.

Watch for: “Not needed today” is not equivalent to “not required.” The 100-hour inspection overrun is for reaching an inspection location, not an extra local lesson.

Weather reports, forecasts and decisions

ACS study group: PA.I.C

Start with the observation time and the period you expect to fly. Decode the report, then compare departure, en-route and arrival conditions with terrain, legal requirements, your personal minimums and available alternatives. A flight-category color alone is not a briefing.

Try this case

A hypothetical observation is 6SM SCT015 BKN035. The arrival forecast also has a temporary period of 2SM BKN009 overlapping your ETA. Which layer is the observed ceiling, and why does the temporary forecast matter?

Show the explanation

The observed ceiling is the broken layer at 3,500 ft AGL; the scattered 1,500 ft layer is not a ceiling. The forecast temporary conditions are a separate arrival risk: two-mile visibility and a 900 ft ceiling could occur during your arrival window. Compare the forecast with the route and alternatives rather than treating the current six-mile observation as a promise.

Watch for: METAR describes observed conditions at a station and time. TEMPO is part of the forecast; it is not permission to disregard worse conditions.

Cross-country navigation, time and fuel

ACS study group: PA.I.D / PA.VI

Keep true, magnetic and compass directions separate. Use groundspeed for elapsed time and fuel planning, then update the plan from actual checkpoint progress. Cruise fuel is only one part of the budget; start, taxi, climb, diversion and reserve allowances need their own treatment.

Try this case

A 30 NM checkpoint leg takes 20 minutes. There are 45 NM left and the simplified cruise burn is 8 gal/hr. Assuming the observed speed continues, estimate the remaining cruise time and fuel.

Show the explanation

Observed groundspeed is 30 ÷ (20/60) = 90 kt. Remaining time is 45/90 = 0.5 hour, or 30 minutes. The remaining cruise burn is 0.5 × 8 = 4 gallons. Add required reserves and other allowances separately; if wind or routing changes, recalculate rather than preserving the original ETA.

Watch for: Distance divided by knots gives hours, not minutes. A heading points the nose; a course describes the desired ground track.

Airspace and airport operations

ACS study group: PA.I.E / PA.III

Read the vertical limits at your actual horizontal position and use the stated altitude reference. Before entering, check the relevant communication or clearance requirement, weather, equipment and any current restriction. Radar contact alone does not authorize entry into every airspace class.

Try this case

You approach a Class B shelf and receive an assigned squawk plus a callsign acknowledgment. ATC has not issued a Class B clearance. Is it time to enter? What changes if the airspace is Class C and ATC explicitly says “remain outside”?

Show the explanation

Remain outside Class B until the required clearance is received. A callsign acknowledgment or transponder code is not that clearance. Class C normally uses a two-way-communication entry requirement, but an explicit instruction to remain outside still controls. The airspace class and the actual instruction both matter.

Watch for: Do not apply the Class C callsign test to Class B. A charted MSL floor is not height above the ground.

Loading, CG and aircraft performance

ACS study group: PA.I.F

Identify the exact chart, aircraft configuration and units before calculating. Check loaded weight and CG against the aircraft’s envelope, including relevant changes during flight. Distinguish takeoff ground roll from total obstacle-clearance distance, and apply the published corrections.

Try this case

A hypothetical training table gives 800 ft ground roll at 10°C and 1,000 ft at 20°C. Its instructions permit linear interpolation. What is the 15°C estimate, and can you use it as distance over a 50 ft obstacle?

Show the explanation

Fifteen degrees is halfway through the temperature interval. Add half the 200 ft distance difference to 800 ft: the estimate is 900 ft ground roll. It is not the total distance over an obstacle. Use the correct obstacle-distance data, actual aircraft conditions, prescribed corrections and an appropriate margin.

Watch for: Being below maximum weight does not establish acceptable CG. A generic interpolation is not a substitute for the actual POH/AFM procedure.

Aircraft systems, instruments and aerodynamics

ACS study group: PA.I.G / PA.VII

Trace the source of each indication rather than memorizing only a failure label. Know which equipment depends on the electrical, pitot-static or vacuum system in your particular airplane. For aerodynamic questions, keep angle of attack, load factor and indicated speed distinct.

Try this case

A conventional magnetic-ignition piston airplane’s electrical master is off. A person concludes that moving the propeller cannot produce ignition. What is missing from that reasoning?

Show the explanation

Engine-driven magnetos can generate ignition power independently of the battery and master switch. Master off therefore does not prove ignition is disabled, especially if an ignition switch or grounding circuit is faulty. Treat the propeller using the approved safety procedures; this explanation is not a hand-propping instruction.

Watch for: The master switch does not mechanically lock the engine. A pitot blockage with its drain open behaves differently from an inlet-and-drain blockage.

Human factors and decisions under pressure

ACS study group: PA.I.H

Assess your condition before judging the airplane or route. Fatigue, stress, illness and medication can affect attention and judgment. Preselected personal minimums are most useful when they remain meaningful after passengers or a deadline start pushing the decision.

Try this case

You planned to delay below your personal ceiling minimum. The forecast now falls below it, and a passenger needs to reach an appointment. What new information would actually justify changing the decision?

Show the explanation

A passenger’s deadline changes the consequences of delay, not the weather or your capability. Reassess the route, terrain, forecast, proficiency and alternatives. A considered revision needs defensible evidence about risk; lowering the limit solely to preserve the schedule defeats the reason you chose it before the pressure developed.

Watch for: Feeling confident is not proof of unimpaired performance. Coffee is not evidence that lost sleep has stopped affecting judgment.

Emergency planning and night operations

ACS study group: PA.IX / PA.X / PA.XI

Keep control of the airplane and a viable landing plan while applying the approved procedure. For glide planning, use height above the candidate landing area, not MSL altitude alone. Wind, turns, terrain and the height needed to land reduce what an ideal still-air calculation suggests.

Try this case

You are at 5,500 ft MSL and the landing area is at 2,500 ft MSL. Using a hypothetical 8:1 glide ratio, estimate still-air horizontal reach before maneuvering allowances. How would a headwind affect it?

Show the explanation

Available height is 3,000 ft. Multiply by eight to obtain 24,000 horizontal feet, then divide by about 6,076 ft/NM: approximately 3.95 NM. A headwind reduces ground reach for the same airspeed and descent performance. Do not treat the calculated edge as a reachable landing guarantee; account for turns, terrain, approach height and aircraft-specific guidance.

Watch for: Using all 5,500 ft ignores the landing area’s elevation. Do not let troubleshooting consume the attention needed for control and the landing plan.

Private Pilot ACS topics

The Private Pilot for Airplane Category ACS (FAA-S-ACS-6C) covers knowledge, risk management and flying skill. Pick a route and airplane, then work through the applicable regulations, weather, loading and performance. Be ready to explain your decisions.

Subjects to review

  • Airworthiness, required documents, inspections, equipment, and pilot privileges and limitations.
  • Weather products and the difference between reading a report and making a decision from it.
  • Cross-country planning: course, wind correction, groundspeed, time, fuel, alternates, airspace, and runway considerations.
  • Weight and balance and performance using the actual aircraft data, not generic numbers.
  • Aerodynamics, stalls, load factor, maneuvering speed, and the reasons behind the maneuver standards.
  • Emergency planning, diversion thinking, systems knowledge, and risk management.
