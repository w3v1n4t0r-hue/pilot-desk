Aviation temperature conversions

Aviation weather and performance work commonly uses degrees Celsius, while many everyday U.S. references use Fahrenheit. Engineering equations may require Kelvin. Converting the unit correctly is especially important for density altitude, true airspeed and speed-of-sound calculations.

Celsius to Fahrenheit

Use °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32. For example, 20°C becomes 68°F. Freezing is 0°C or 32°F, which is a useful mental check.

Fahrenheit to Celsius

Use °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9. A temperature of 86°F becomes 30°C.

Celsius and Kelvin

Kelvin uses the same size degree as Celsius but starts at absolute zero. K = °C + 273.15. A temperature of 15°C is 288.15 K. Kelvin is often required in physics equations because it is an absolute temperature scale.

Why aviation often uses Celsius

METAR temperature and dew point are reported in Celsius, and standard-atmosphere relationships are commonly expressed in Celsius. Aircraft performance charts may use Celsius even when other aircraft instruments display Fahrenheit.

Do not convert temperature differences like absolute temperatures

A change of 1°C equals a change of 1 K and equals a change of 1.8°F. When dealing with a temperature difference rather than an actual temperature, the +32 offset does not apply in the same way.

Check the chart label

Before entering temperature into a calculator or POH chart, verify whether the source expects °C or °F. Entering 30°F as though it were 30°C creates a major performance error.

How Aviation temperature conversions fits into pilot training

Learn Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin conversions for aviation weather, performance and technical calculations with simple examples.

Unit conversions are simple only when the starting quantity is identified correctly. Write the unit next to every value, convert one quantity at a time, and perform a reasonableness check before moving the number into another formula. A conversion changes the unit, not the type of speed, distance, pressure, temperature, weight or volume being described.

A practical way to study this topic

Start by naming the quantity you are trying to find and the units it should have. Write down the source of each input, make a rough mental estimate, and then work the relationship. If the calculated answer is far outside the estimate, check units, signs, direction references and whether the formula assumes groundspeed, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, pressure altitude or another specific value.

For an actual flight, finish the process by comparing the training result with the current source that controls the operation. That may be the aircraft POH or AFM, an FAA or charting publication, current airport data, official weather, ATC instructions, or an operator procedure. The purpose of the math is to improve understanding and cross-checking—not to replace those sources.
